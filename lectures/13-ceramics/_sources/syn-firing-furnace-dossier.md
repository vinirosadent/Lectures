## Q1. Porcelain furnace anatomy; furnace types

- A porcelain furnace "consists, essentially, of an electrically heated muffle with a pyrometer" [McCabe & Walls, *Applied Dental Materials* 9th ed (tagged "Applied Dental Materials 9e" on pocketdentistry.com, ch. 11 "Ceramics and Porcelain Fused to Metal (PFM)"); TB]. URL: https://pocketdentistry.com/11-ceramics-and-porcelain-fused-to-metal-pfm/
- "Modern furnaces are usually computer controlled". Firing is done "in air or a partial vacuum, producing an atmosphere about 10% of normal" [*A Clinical Guide to Applied Dental Materials* (Elsevier; ISBN in image IDs 9780702031588), ch. 22 "Dental ceramics"; TB]. URL: https://pocketdentistry.com/22-dental-ceramics/
- **Heating element, porcelain furnace (technical example):**
  - A dental-furnace patent describes the heater as "a coil-shaped resistant heating element made of an alloy such as Fe–Cr–Al alloy", with "a quartz sleeve … disposed inward of heating element" [Zychek GN, US 6,252,202 B1, Jeneric/Pentron, granted 26 Jun 2001; TECH]. URL: https://patents.google.com/patent/US6252202
  - FeCrAl is the alloy family sold as Kanthal. The patent names the alloy but not the brand.
  - The same patent's background describes bottom-loading furnaces (the work is raised into an upper heating chamber), front-loading furnaces (cold spots at the front) and uneven heating, because the piece sits on an unheated insulation block.
- **Typical example, porcelain/glaze/crystallisation furnace** [Ivoclar Vivadent, Programat P510 brochure, doc 664378/en, 2017-01-19; MAN]. URL: https://zoolu.ivoclar.com/zoolu-website/media/document/21963/Programat+P510
  - "Max. firing temperature 1200 °C"
  - Heating rate up to 140 °C/min
  - Final vacuum "< 50 mbar" (≈50 hPa, about 5% of atmospheric pressure)
  - Muffle: "QTK2 muffle technology with SiC bottom reflector"
  - Chamber 90 mm Ø × 80 mm high
  - Automatic temperature calibration (ATK2)
  - An infrared camera controls pre-drying, which the brochure says "Reduces cracks or fractures in the ceramic"
  - The brochure does not state the heating-element material.
- **Typical example, zirconia sintering furnaces:**
  - Programat S1 1600: "Max. sintering temperature 1600 °C"; programs have three heating stages and two cooling stages; speed sintering takes "about 75 minutes" for a ZirCAD coping in one place and "90 Min instead 5–8 h" in another (inconsistent within the brochure) [Ivoclar, doc 680874/en/2017-02; MAN]. The brochure does not state the element material. URL: https://zoolu.ivoclar.com/zoolu-website/media/document/33219/Programat+S1+1600
  - Nabertherm LHT 17 D: "heating elements made of molybdenum disilicide", Tmax 1650 °C, "Type S thermocouple" [Nabertherm product page; MAN]. URL: https://nabertherm.com/en/products/dental/sintering-furnaces/sintering-furnaces-lht-17-d-1650-degc
- **Review statement on sintering-furnace elements:** "Conventional furnaces contain heating resistance elements, molybdenum disilicate [sic]… Resistance elements limit the heating rates of the furnace to 40 to 70 °C per min". Induction (high-speed) furnaces heat the zirconia through a susceptor in an alternating magnetic field. One cited high-speed protocol is a 10 min hold at 1570–1590 °C, heating at 50–100 °C/min [Al-Haj Husain N et al., J Clin Med 2022;11:4892, Introduction; REV]. DOI 10.3390/jcm11164892
- **Pressing furnaces:** the heat-pressing temperature is "chosen near the softening point of the ceramic", and "A pressure of 0.3 to 0.4 MPa is then applied through a refractory plunger" [Craig's *Restorative Dental Materials* 13th ed (Sakaguchi & Powers, tagged "Craigs Restorative Dental Materials 13e"), ch. 11; TB]. URL: https://pocketdentistry.com/11-restorative-materials-ceramics/ — The maximum temperature of a specific press furnace was NOT VERIFIED (no document opened).
- **Why the work is dried at the furnace entrance:**
  - "It is important that the mass is slowly heated initially to eliminate the water from the slurry". The mass is "usually held near the entrance to the furnace for sometime before being introduced inside" [Clinical Guide, ch. 22; TB].
  - "The normal procedure… is to dry the wet structure in a warm atmosphere before placing into the hot furnace". "At the elevated temperatures of the furnace, the starch or sugar binder ignites and the surface of the structure blackens" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB].
  - [INF] Rapid heating of a wet compact turns the water to steam inside it, which can crack or blow apart the layer. This matches the P510 claim that controlled pre-drying reduces cracks.
- **Thermocouple type in porcelain furnaces:** NOT VERIFIED. Type S is verified only for the Nabertherm sintering furnace above.

## Q2. Porcelain firing cycle: stages and what each does

- **Classification by firing temperature.** Phillips' *Science of Dental Materials*, ch. 10 "Ceramic-Based Materials", Table 10-2 [TB; edition not shown on the page, likely 13th]. URL: https://pocketdentistry.com/ceramic-based-materials/

  | Class | Firing range | Uses |
  |---|---|---|
  | High fusing | >1300 °C | Denture teeth; fully sintered alumina and zirconia cores |
  | Medium fusing | 1101–1300 °C | Denture teeth; presintered zirconia |
  | Low fusing | 850–1100 °C | Crown and bridge veneer ceramic |
  | Ultralow fusing | <850 °C | Crown and bridge veneer ceramic |

  - The ranges agree with McCabe & Walls 9e (high fusing 1300–1400 °C, low fusing 850–1100 °C) and with the Clinical Guide (ultra-low <850 °C, low 850–1100 °C).
  - **Conflict:** a dental-assistant textbook (*Dental Materials: Clinical Applications for Dental Assistants and Dental Hygienists*, pocketdentistry ch. 8/9) gives older ranges: high 1288/1294–1371 °C, medium 1093–1260 °C, low 871–1066 °C. Use the Phillips table.
- **Biscuit (bisque) stages:**
  - First bake: furnace "set at about 50 °C below the fusing temperature"; the result is "the biscuit bake" [Clinical Guide ch. 22; TB].
  - Cheung & Darvell (2002) mapped sintering time against temperature for five dentine porcelains (750–1100 °C, 0–1000 min). They delineated an acceptable-appearance window ("high biscuit to not quite slumped"). The window boundaries are related to the activation energy of diffusive processes [Cheung KC, Darvell BW, Dent Mater 2002;18:163–73; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/s0109-5641(01)00038-0
  - The named low/medium/high bisque descriptors (surface texture and glaze appearance at each stage) were NOT VERIFIED from an opened textbook.
- **Time versus temperature:** "The reduction of porosity of dentine porcelain is much more sensitive to temperature than to time". "Minimum porosity was obtained at high temperature and short time" [Cheung & Darvell 2002; EXP].
- **Vacuum application and release:**
  - "Much of the air within the ceramic structure is removed as the vacuum develops… The little that remains will be below atmospheric pressure". When "the vacuum is released… this results in the voids collapsing" [Clinical Guide ch. 22; TB].
  - Vacuum firing "has the effect of reducing the porosity within the finished material from around 4.6% to about 0.5%" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB]. The primary source of these numbers is not given on the page.
  - Typical modern vacuum: <50 mbar [P510; MAN]. "About 10% of normal" atmosphere [Clinical Guide; TB].
  - [INF] Mechanism for teaching: gas trapped in a closed pore resists further shrinkage of that pore. A pore sealed under ~5–10% of atmospheric pressure holds little gas. Restoring 1 atm outside, while the glass is still viscous, adds a pressure difference that helps the pore shrink.
- **Vacuum during lithium disilicate crystallisation** (typical example): Vacuum 1 at 550–820 °C and Vacuum 2 at 820–840 °C [IPS e.max CAD Chairside IFU; MAN] (see Q7).
- **Heating rates, typical example:** up to 140 °C/min (porcelain furnace, P510) [MAN]; 40–70 °C/min (MoSi2 sintering furnaces) [Al-Haj Husain 2022; REV]. A heating rate typical for veneering porcelain from a textbook was NOT VERIFIED.
- **Cooling:** "It is important that once this firing cycle has been completed, the ceramic is allowed to cool slowly and uniformly. Stresses can lead to cracking and a loss in strength due to thermal shock" [Clinical Guide ch. 22; TB]. "Whenever porcelain work is heated or cooled the process must be carried out slowly" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB].
- **Firing shrinkage of porcelain (values conflict):**
  - "in the range of 10–20%" [Clinical Guide; TB]
  - "up to 10% linearly" [pocketdentistry "14. Dental Ceramics"; probably Phillips 12th ed, unconfirmed; TB]
  - "about 25% to 40%" [dental-assistant text; TB]
  - "about 15% to 20%" for the core material of early all-ceramic systems [Phillips ch. 10; TB]
  - [INF] The likely source of conflict is that some figures are linear and others volumetric, and they come from different condensation and packing states. Say "roughly 15–20% linear, so restorations are built oversize", or check the printed textbook.

## Q3. Viscous (glass-phase) sintering of porcelain powder

- "During sintering, the particles only melt and fuse at their surfaces". "The sintering temperature is often referred to as the fusing temperature of the ceramic" [pocketdentistry "14. Dental Ceramics", Phillips-type text; TB]. "Sintering is the process of firing the compacted ceramic powder at high temperature to ensure optimal densification" [Craig's 13e; TB]. "Shrinkage takes place as the fluxes bind the particles together causing a uniform inward contraction of the whole mass" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB].
- "Mechanical condensation… can only achieve a limited effect — the majority of consolidation and porosity elimination is achieved by sintering" [Cheung & Darvell 2002, abstract; EXP].
- **Physics of viscous sintering** (silicate glass beads, not dental porcelain, but the same mechanism) [Wadsworth FB et al., Proc R Soc A 2016;472:20150780; EXP + theory]. DOI 10.1098/rspa.2015.0780
  - Above Tg, glass droplets sinter by flow "driven by the interfacial tension". The driving stress is the excess (Laplace) pressure 2Γ/R.
  - The kinetics scale with a capillary time ∝ viscosity × length / surface tension. Viscosity falls steeply with temperature, so sintering is far more sensitive to temperature than to time, which matches Cheung & Darvell's dental result.
  - The Frenkel neck-growth model describes the early stage. The Mackenzie–Shuttleworth pore-shrinkage model describes later stages.
  - Once pores become isolated, at ~3 vol% gas, densification stops at a residual trapped-gas fraction. Falling permeability "inhibits late-stage densification".
  - [INF] This is the physical basis for vacuum firing: less gas in pores that close later means less residual porosity.
- **Classic vacuum-firing primary studies:**
  - Vines RF, Semmelman JO. "Densification of dental porcelain." J Dent Res 1957;36(6):950–6. DOI 10.1177/00220345570360062201 — existence verified on PubMed and SAGE. The abstract and full text were NOT accessible, so its findings are NOT VERIFIED.
  - The often-cited "Vines et al. 1958" paper was not found in PubMed or in web search: NOT VERIFIED.
  - Paquet GH 1966, "A comparison of surface-roughness, hardness and porosity of air-fired and vacuum-fired porcelain", Dent Res Grad Study 66(21):14–9 — exists in PubMed, no abstract (findings NOT VERIFIED).
- **Vacuum firing and translucency/strength:**
  - Vacuum firing "reduce[s] porosity and in turn produce[s] a stronger material" [dental-assistant text, pocketdentistry "Dental Ceramics"; TB].
  - A specific primary measurement of translucency gain from vacuum firing was NOT VERIFIED.
- **Porosity and strength, a nuance:** porosity can act as a crack initiator. One study found the amount of porosity had no effect on flexural strength, while clustered or near-surface voids matter [Jansen van Vuuren W-A et al., N Z Dent J 2018;114:13–18, Introduction and Discussion; EXP]. URL: https://assets.nzda.org.nz/files/Archives/NZDJ_Articles/2018/March_2018/The_effect_of_condensing_on_mechanical_properties_of_feldspathic_veneering_porcelain.pdf — No DOI seen.

## Q4. Over-firing, under-firing, multiple firings

- **Over-firing:**
  - "Overfiring can result in molten glass flowing too much and the restoration losing its shape" [Clinical Guide; TB]. "any overheating will cause gross distortions of the shape" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB].
  - Prolonged sintering at high temperature did not lower porosity as theory predicts. Instead "porosity increased markedly in the feldspathic porcelains, particularly VMK-68" [Cheung & Darvell 2002; EXP]. [INF] This is consistent with bloating, i.e., gas expansion in closed pores.
  - The upper acceptable boundary is "not quite slumped" [Cheung & Darvell 2002].
- **Under-firing:** [INF from Cheung & Darvell's time–temperature–porosity maps] Below the biscuit window, porosity remains high. A direct textbook statement on the optical or mechanical consequences of under-firing was NOT VERIFIED.
- **Multiple firings and devitrification:** porcelains "should not be subjected to nonessential repeated firings because this may lead to devitrification", which raises cloudiness and changes the thermal expansion coefficient [Phillips ch. 10; TB].
- **Leucite and CTE (Mackert group):**
  - Leucite's effective linear CTE (25–700 °C) is 28 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹; sanidine's is 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. Leucite → sanidine conversion during heat treatments "would produce a detrimental lowering of the porcelain thermal expansion" [Mackert JR, Twiggs SW, Williams AL, J Dent Res 2000;79:1590–5; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345000790081101
  - Slow cooling inside the closed furnace, versus removal into room air, raised leucite volume fraction in every porcelain tested (by 8.5–55.8%, mean 26.9%). This explains Prasad et al.'s finding that slow cooling raises porcelain expansion enough to make a compatible metal–porcelain pair incompatible [Mackert JR, Evans AL, J Dent Res 1991;70:137–9; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345910700020801
  - Isothermal anneals at 750 °C (4–16 min, as in post-soldering) increased leucite volume fraction by 6.1–21.3%. Anneals at 500 °C had little effect. Baseline leucite fractions in commercial porcelains were 0.155–0.442 [Mackert JR, Twiggs SW, Evans-Williams AL, J Dent Res 1995;74:1259–65; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345950740060401
  - Microcracks around leucite form because of the large CTE mismatch with the glass. A 750 °C/16 min anneal cut microcrack density from 575 to 231 cm²/cm³, predicting a ~6% rise in CTE [Mackert JR et al., J Dent Res 1994;73:1221–7; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345940730061401
  - Up to 16 firings changed microcrack density only negligibly in most porcelains [Mackert JR, Williams AL, J Dent Res 1996;75:1484–90; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345960750070801
  - Leucite undergoes a cubic → tetragonal transformation on cooling with a 1.2% volume contraction, creating microcracks and tangential compressive stresses. Stabilising cubic leucite reduced strength, so these stresses contribute to strengthening [Denry IL, Mackert JR et al., J Dent Res 1996;75:1928–35; EXP]. DOI 10.1177/00220345960750120301
  - Heat-pressed leucite ceramic (IPS Empress): leucite content increased after the first and second colourant firings, then plateaued [Mackert JR, Russell CM, Int J Prosthodont 1996;9:261–5; EXP]. No DOI listed in PubMed (PMID 8957861).
- **Colour after repeated firing:** there was no colour change over the first five firings, air- or vacuum-fired. Slight changes appeared later and were more noticeable in air-fired samples, but were not enough to reject the samples [Barghi N, Goldberg J, J Prosthet Dent 1977;37:173–5; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/0022-3913(77)90239-6
- **Fluorescence after multiple firings:** NOT VERIFIED (no source found).

## Q5. Glazing

- **Self-glaze (natural glaze):** "the surface of the normal porcelain will flow and glaze with only a slight rounding of the contours" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB]. In the self-glaze firing, "A thin external layer of glassy material is formed" [Phillips ch. 10; TB].
- **Overglaze (add-on glaze):** "A low-fusing, transparent glass may be used as a glaze over the completed body" [McCabe & Walls 9e; TB]. The add-on glaze slurry "contains more glass modifiers" and fires lower. "To ensure adequate chemical durability, a self-glaze of porcelain is preferred to an add-on glaze" [Phillips ch. 10; TB].
- **Strength effects (sources disagree):**
  - "Autoglazed feldspathic porcelain is stronger than unglazed porcelain" [Phillips ch. 10; TB].
  - Self-glazing "does not significantly improve the flexural strength of feldspathic dental porcelains". When glaze is applied, "Upon cooling, this glaze layer is placed under compression from the greater contraction of the underlying ceramic" [Craig's 13e; TB].
  - Experimental: for IPS Empress and Empress 2 discs, "Heat treatment [glaze] had no effect on roughness or strength values"; polished groups were strongest [Albakry M, Guazzato M, Swain MV, J Dent 2004;32:91–9; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.jdent.2003.08.006
  - See the conflicts list.

## Q6. Cooling rate and residual stress (porcelain on zirconia)

- **Tempering:** McCabe & Walls 9e describe compressive surface stress forming when the inner material solidifies later "during the initial firing of the ceramic or during subsequent heat tempering" [TB]. Craig's 13e states that tempering techniques, though "widely used in the glass industry", are not used for dental ceramics [TB]. **Conflict in emphasis:** present tempering as glass-industry physics, not dental lab practice.
- **Thermal compatibility:** systems with a thermal-contraction-coefficient difference ≤0.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ are "thermally compatible"; differences of 0.5–1.0 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ often survive for years [Phillips ch. 10; TB].
- **Swain 2009 (analysis, PROPOSED mechanism)** [Swain MV, Acta Biomater 2009;5:1668–77; theoretical analysis]. DOI 10.1016/j.actbio.2008.12.016
  - Residual stresses from fabrication, coupled with contact-induced cracking, are proposed as the origin of the excess clinical chipping.
  - The key parameters are thermo-elastic properties and layer thicknesses.
  - Porcelain fused to glass-ceramic and to zirconia substrates are "most prone".
  - Proposes an approach to minimise chipping.
- **Tholey, Swain & Thiel 2011 (DEMONSTRATED in vitro)** [Dent Mater 2011;27:1102–10; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.dental.2011.08.001
  - Thermocouples measured inner-to-outer temperature differences in veneered Y-TZP crowns.
  - Fast cooling gave >100 °C (uniform framework) and >140 °C (anatomical framework) above Tg.
  - Slow cooling to Tg (600 °C) before removal reduced but did not eliminate the gradient (up to 88 °C).
  - Polarimetry showed much lower porcelain residual stress after slow cooling.
  - The anatomical framework did not benefit to the same extent.
- **Guazzato et al. 2010 (DEMONSTRATED)** [Aust Dent J 2010;55:306–10; EXP]. DOI 10.1111/j.1834-7819.2010.01239.x — On zirconia spheres, spontaneous cracks and rupture in the veneer increased with faster cooling and thicker veneer in 3 of 5 "nominally compatible" porcelain–zirconia combinations.
- **Zhang Z, Guazzato M et al. 2013 (MODELLED, XFEM)** [Acta Biomater 2013;9:8394–402]. DOI 10.1016/j.actbio.2013.05.009 — Fast cooling can thermally fracture bilayers; heat-transfer-coefficient thresholds were defined.
- **Rues et al. 2010 (DEMONSTRATED, partly)** [J Dent 2010;38:987–94; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.jdent.2010.08.014 — Adding 6 min of cooling time increased resistance of veneered anterior zirconia crowns. This was significant for only one of two veneering ceramics.
- **Tang et al. 2017 (DEMONSTRATED)** [J Adv Prosthodont 2017;9:152–8; EXP]. DOI 10.4047/jap.2017.9.3.152 — Slow cooling combined with a 1.5 mm coping gave the highest failure load after fatigue.
- **Contrary evidence:** de Lima et al. 2022 found "no effect of the cooling protocol" on edge-chipping resistance or fracture toughness for layered or pressed veneers on 3Y-TZP bars [J Mech Behav Biomed Mater 2022;126:104995; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.jmbbm.2021.104995
- **Zirconia's low thermal diffusivity as the cause:** widely stated, but the opened abstracts say "thermo-elastic properties" (Swain) and "temperature gradients" (Tholey). No opened source gave a diffusivity value. Treat "low thermal diffusivity" as NOT VERIFIED wording; "steep temperature gradients across the crown during cooling" is verified (Tholey 2011).
- **Overall verdict:** slow cooling lowering gradients and residual stress is demonstrated in vitro. Its effect on chipping resistance is inconsistent across tests. Its clinical benefit is proposed but not proven.

## Q7. Lithium disilicate (IPS e.max CAD) crystallisation firing

- **Microstructural pathway (in situ XRD)** [Ortiz AL, Borrero-López O, Guiberteau F, Zhang Y, Dent Mater 2019;35:697–708; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.dental.2019.02.011 (PMC6462418, NIH author manuscript)
  - The blue (pre-crystallised) block is ~40 vol% crystals: mainly Li2SiO3 rods 0.5–1.5 µm, with minor Li3PO4 and Li2Si2O5, in glass.
  - On heating, the structure is unchanged until ~660 °C, when SiO2 (cristobalite) crystallises from the glass.
  - At ~735–760 °C, the solid-state reaction Li2SiO3 + SiO2 → Li2Si2O5 runs (complete at ~770 °C).
  - CeO2 precipitates at the surface (outer 1–3 µm) from ~775 °C.
  - After heating to 900 °C the material is interlocked Li2Si2O5 rods up to ~20 µm × 3 µm, at ~70–80 vol%.
  - Holding at 850 °C gives coarser rods (<5 × 2 µm) than holding at 770 °C (<2 × 0.8 µm). Microstructure is tunable by the firing cycle.
  - The introduction states "crystal density (up to ~70 vol.%)… strength (460–500 MPa)… toughness (2.1–2.5 MPa·m½)" (cited, not measured here).
  - **Manufacturer cycle as reported by Ortiz (typical example):** 60 °C/min to ~770 °C (10 s), then 30 °C/min to ~850 °C, hold ~10 min [MAN-derived].
- **Manufacturer data (typical example)** [Ivoclar Vivadent, *Scientific Documentation IPS e.max CAD*, March 2011; MAN]. URL: https://zoolu.ivoclar.com/zoolu-website/media/document/9793/IPS+e-max+CAD
  - Partially crystallised: "40% lithium metasilicate crystals (Li2SiO3)", 0.2–1.0 µm.
  - Fully crystallised: "approx. 70% fine-grain lithium disilicate crystals"; the document does not say vol% or wt%.
  - "Linear shrinkage during tempering 0.2%".
  - Biaxial strength: 130 ± 30 MPa (partially crystallised) → 360 ± 60 MPa (fully crystallised).
  - Colour: blue → "desired tooth colour and opacity during tempering".
  - Crystallised "at 850 °C" (figure caption).
- **Chairside IFU programme (typical example)** [IPS e.max CAD Chairside instructions for use, version and date not shown; MAN]. URL: https://cms.cws.net/content/trachseldentalstudio.com/files/IPS%2Be-max%2BCAD%2BChairside.pdf
  - Stand-by 403 °C; closing 6 min
  - 90 °C/min to 820 °C (10 s); 30 °C/min to 840 °C, hold 7 min
  - Vacuum 550–820 °C and 820–840 °C
  - Long-term cooling to 700 °C
  - Restorations "densif[y] by about 0.2%", which the CAD software compensates for
  - Firing paste "prevents warping"; long-term cooling gives "tension-free cooling"
- **Previously verified dossier (not re-opened here):** Zhang Y et al., JDR 2023 (lithia glass-ceramics), p. 247: crystallisation shrinkage "~0.2% vs. ~20% sintering shrinkage of zirconia", attributed to glass viscosity staying high throughout crystallisation. See zhang2023-lithia-glass-ceramics-jdr.md.
- **Conflicts:**
  - Ortiz reports the manufacturer cycle as 770/850 °C with a 10 min hold; the chairside IFU uses 820/840 °C with a 7 min hold. These are different programmes and versions; give students a range (~840–850 °C).
  - The 40%/70% figures from the manufacturer have no basis stated; Ortiz measured ~40 vol% and ~70–80 vol%.

## Q8. Zirconia sintering

- **Pre-sintered (soft-machined) blanks and shrinkage:**
  - Soft-milled restorations "should be milled to a 25% larger size to compensate for sintering shrinkage". The conventional cycle is 6–8 h including heating, dwell and cooling; the furnace must reach ~1500 °C. "Fully sintered zirconia is approximately twice as dense as pre-sintered zirconia" [Al-Haj Husain 2022; REV].
  - Craig's 13e: machined zirconia shrinks about 25% during sintering, compensated by enlarging the design [TB].
  - Previously verified dossiers (not re-opened): Zhang 2023 JDR, ~20% sintering shrinkage; Aljohani 2024, "20–35% volumetric shrinkage".
  - [INF, arithmetic] Milling 25% oversize corresponds to 20% linear shrinkage (1/1.25 = 0.80). A 20% linear shrinkage equals ~49% volumetric shrinkage (0.8³ = 0.51). The quoted "20–35% volumetric" therefore cannot correspond to 20–25% linear. Flag this inconsistency; prefer "~20–25% linear".
- **Temperature and hold time: grain size, strength, translucency:**
  - **Stawarczyk et al.** [Clin Oral Investig 2013;17:269–74 (epub 2012); EXP]. DOI 10.1007/s00784-012-0692-6
    - Ceramill ZI was sintered at 1300–1700 °C with a 120 min hold.
    - Highest flexural strength at 1400–1550 °C; highest Weibull modulus at 1400 °C.
    - Grain size rose with temperature; defects appeared above 1650 °C.
    - The authors recommend "should not exceed 1,550 °C".
    - (Contrast-ratio direction: see conflicts.)
  - **Juntavee & Attashu** [J Clin Exp Dent 2018;10:e821–30; EXP]. DOI 10.4317/jced.54749 — For translucent monolithic Y-TZP, 1350/1450/1550 °C × 60/120/180 min: strength rose with temperature and time (~1080 → ~1600 MPa). Grains enlarged, and t→m shifting increased.
  - **Kim MJ et al.** [J Adv Prosthodont 2013;5:161–6; EXP]. DOI 10.4047/jap.2013.5.2.161 — Density did not differ between conditions. Grain size rose from 347 → 1512 nm (Lava) as conventional hold went 20 min → 40 h. Shorter sintering gave smaller grains and higher transmittance (28–34%).
- **Speed and high-speed sintering:**
  - **Systematic review** [Al-Haj Husain 2022; REV; 15 in-vitro studies, no clinical studies]: speed and high-speed sintering give similar or better mechanical and fit results, but translucency is usually reduced for 3Y-TZP. Lawson et al. (cited) found large grain growth (1.24 → 4.11 µm) and pores in two 5Y materials.
  - **Lawson & Maharishi** [J Esthet Restor Dent 2020;32:219–25; EXP]. DOI 10.1111/jerd.12524 — High-speed sintering (18–30 min) versus 7 h: Prettau Anterior and Zpex Smile grew larger grains and pores and lost translucency and strength; Katana STML was unaffected.
  - **Liu, Inokoshi et al.** [Dent Mater 2022;38:451–68; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.dental.2021.12.028 — Speed (~90 min) versus conventional (~7 h) for 5 Y-PSZ products: no significant change in translucency or strength. Speed-sintered material had smaller grains and lower c-ZrO2 in most materials. LTD (134 °C autoclave) was similar.
  - **Luz et al.** [Dent Mater 2021;37:875–81; EXP]. DOI 10.1016/j.dental.2021.02.026
    - Microwave 1450 °C/15 min versus conventional 1530 °C/2 h for 3Y-TZP.
    - Grain size 0.53 vs 0.89 µm; cubic 15.3% vs 22.7%; translucency parameter 13 vs 29.
    - Density 5.98 vs 6.03 g/cm³; strength similar (978 vs 1044 MPa).
  - **Soult et al.** [Gen Dent 2019;67(5):30–4; EXP; no DOI in PubMed, PMID 31454319] — Induction furnace 26.2 min versus conventional 4.3 h: smaller grains, with no difference in strength or optical properties.
- **Grain size, cubic phase, LTD and translucency** [Zhang Y, Lawn BR, J Dent Res 2018;97:140–7; REV]. DOI 10.1177/0022034517737483
  - First- and second-generation 3Y-TZP grains are "~0.5 to 1 µm"; opacity arises from birefringence of non-cubic phases.
  - Third-generation material has a "larger grain size (~1.5 µm), with greater content of c phase".
  - "cubic zirconia does not undergo stress-induced transformation", which lowers strength and toughness.
  - "LTD can be mitigated by maintaining a smaller grain size with homogeneous yttria distribution". "Zirconias with higher c content are less susceptible to aging".
  - "a coarser microstructure generally diminishes S but enhances T".
  - <100 nm grains are needed for good transmittance in Y-TZP.
- **Association between hotter/longer sintering and cubic content:** supported by Luz 2021 and Liu 2022 (less cubic after shorter or cooler cycles). The yttrium-partitioning mechanism was NOT VERIFIED from an opened source.

## Q9. Reusable figures and what a correct schematic should contain

- **No CC BY dental-furnace or firing-cycle figure was verified.** Wikimedia Commons was blocked by the proxy. Ortiz 2019 (PMC) is an NIH author manuscript, not CC BY.
- **Candidates, with licence to check on the publisher page before reuse:**
  - Wadsworth et al. 2016, Proc R Soc A (open access, PMC4892276): schematic of viscous sintering of packed glass spheres (neck growth to isolated bubbles). Good for Q3.
  - Al-Haj Husain et al. 2022, J Clin Med (MDPI journals are normally CC BY 4.0): contains a PRISMA flowchart only, so not useful.
- **A correct firing-furnace schematic should show:**
  1. A vertical bottom-loading muffle (refractory insulation) with a resistance coil (FeCrAl for porcelain; MoSi2 for >1500 °C sintering), behind a quartz sleeve.
  2. A thermocouple near the work, wired to a programmable PID controller.
  3. A vacuum line to the pump, with a gauge (<50 mbar typical) and a vent valve.
  4. A motorised lift or firing table carrying the tray and firing pegs or pins (or a honeycomb tray for porcelain; crystallisation tray with paste for LDS; sintering tray or beads for zirconia).
  5. The drying position at the furnace mouth.
- **A firing-cycle graph (temperature vs time) should show:**
  1. Pre-dry or closing time at stand-by temperature (~400 °C typical example).
  2. Heating ramp with vacuum ON.
  3. Vacuum OFF near or at final temperature (atmospheric pressure restored).
  4. Hold at maturation temperature.
  5. Cooling, with a "long-term cooling to ~Tg" branch (e.g., 700 °C for LDS; ~600 °C Tg in Tholey 2011).
  6. Overlay three curves on one axis: veneering porcelain (~low-fusing range), LDS crystallisation (~840–850 °C), zirconia sintering (~1450–1550 °C, multi-hour; speed variants ≤~1600 °C).

## Not verified / conflicting

1. Vines et al. 1958 vacuum-firing study: not found. Vines & Semmelman 1957 exists, but its content was not accessible.
2. Numeric gain in translucency or strength from vacuum firing (primary data): not verified. The 4.6% → 0.5% porosity figure is from the McCabe & Walls textbook, primary source unnamed.
3. Porcelain firing shrinkage: 10–20%, "up to 10% linear", 15–20% and 25–40% across textbooks. The linear/volumetric basis is often unstated.
4. Zirconia shrinkage: ~20% (Zhang 2023), ~25% (Craig's; 25% oversize milling in Al-Haj Husain). Aljohani's "20–35% volumetric" is arithmetically incompatible with 20–25% linear.
5. Stawarczyk 2013 abstract is internally inconsistent. It says contrast ratio "higher with the higher sintering temperature", yet reports r = −0.96 between temperature and contrast ratio. The full text could not be read (Springer rate-limited). Do not state the translucency direction from this paper until checked.
6. Self-glaze and strength: Phillips says autoglazed is stronger than unglazed; Craig's says self-glaze does not significantly improve flexural strength; Albakry 2004 found no effect of glaze heat treatment on Empress materials.
7. Slow cooling: reduces gradients and stress (Tholey 2011; Guazzato 2010), but de Lima 2022 found no effect on chipping resistance. Clinical benefit is unproven.
8. "Zirconia's low thermal diffusivity" is a common phrase; no opened source gave it explicitly or with a value.
9. Fluorescence change with repeated firings: no source found.
10. e.max CAD cycle: 770/850 °C with a 10 min hold (as reported by Ortiz) versus 820/840 °C with a 7 min hold (chairside IFU). Manufacturer crystal percentages have no stated vol/wt basis.
11. Named low/medium/high bisque descriptors, typical porcelain heating rates (°C/min) from a textbook, porcelain-furnace thermocouple type, press-furnace maximum temperature, and the Kanthal brand specifically: not verified.
12. Textbook editions and authors were inferred from pocketdentistry tags. Confirm against print: McCabe & Walls 9e; Craig's 13e; Phillips (13th?); *A Clinical Guide to Applied Dental Materials*.
