## 2. Evidence type

Consensus/guidance document (Academy of Dental Materials Guidance Project), labelled "Review". Narrative, principle-oriented; no systematic search; no new data. Aim (abstract, p. 1192 [PDF 1]): "summarize the aspects related to fatigue degradation of dental ceramics, reviewing the concepts of fatigue testing and furthermore aims to provide practical guidance to young scientists entering into fatigue related research."

## 3. Key mechanistic statements (with page)

The paper does **not** formally label claims as demonstrated vs proposed. Labels below follow the paper's own wording: **DOCUMENTED** = paper says "documented/demonstrated/shown/measured" or cites experimental work; **HEDGED/PROPOSED** = paper uses "possibly", "conceivably", "may", "believed"; **GUIDANCE** = authors' recommendation/opinion.

### Brittle fracture, flaws, strength (Lecture §5, also §1)
- Clinical failure of dental ceramics is "usually reported as partial fracture of the restoration (chipping) or as catastrophic fracture of the whole structure." (p. 1192 [PDF 1], abstract) — DOCUMENTED (clinical reports referred to generally).
- "In contrast to metals, ceramics are linear-elastic, brittle materials exhibiting extremely low damage tolerance to failure." (p. 1192 [PDF 1]).
- Fracture "often occurs at loads far below their fracture strength due to intrinsic fatigue degradation via slow crack growth or cyclic fatigue mechanisms" — "Well documented clinical and lab reports have shown" this (p. 1192 [PDF 1]) — DOCUMENTED.
- "The presence and development of surface flaws have a dominant role in damage accumulation and lifetime reduction of ceramic structures." (p. 1192 [PDF 1]).
- Fracture strength of ceramics is "inversely proportional to the largest or critical flaw present in the loaded volume, as described by Griffith's law [1]" (p. 1193 [PDF 2]).
- Strength sensitive to surface defects "and their subsurface extension – the larger the flaw, the lower the strength" → statistical distribution of flaws → high scatter; polishing gives narrower distribution but Weibull statistics remain "mandatory" (p. 1196 [PDF 5]) — GUIDANCE.

### Fatigue definition, SCG vs cyclic (Lecture §5)
- Components in service are loaded far below critical load continuously or repetitively → "fatigue", defined as "the degradation (weakening) of a structural component under the influence of mechanical, chemical or biological stress – and in most cases – a combination of them." (p. 1193 [PDF 2]).
- Flaws grow at service loads below fracture strength; "As the stress intensity at the crack tip increases with growing flaw size, the relation between flaw size and service life becomes exponential." (p. 1193 [PDF 2], Fig. 1).
- Fatigue/endurance limits "may exist at a stress below which no further crack growth happens and failure will not occur no matter how many loading cycles … or how long a component is statically loaded (threshold value)." (p. 1193 [PDF 2]) — HEDGED ("may exist").
- Fast fracture = "critical"; slow growth of cracks = "sub-critical" crack growth (SCG) (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- Two mechanisms at ambient temperature: stress corrosion (SCG, "chemically-assisted by water") and/or cyclic effects (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- SCG "demonstrated 70 years ago [5]"; previously believed no additional cyclic effect in brittle ceramics; now "common understanding that cyclic effects contribute to overall degradation of brittle ceramics, although to lesser extent compared to SCG [3]." (p. 1194 [PDF 3]) — DOCUMENTED (SCG) / consensus statement (cyclic contribution smaller).
- "while SCG might occur in a comparable rate independent from static, dynamic or cyclic loading, cyclic effects arise from friction and hydrolytic pressure during crack closing." (p. 1194 [PDF 3]) — HEDGED ("might").
- SCG is "probably the most important (and most studied …) fatigue mechanism affecting all dental ceramics"; involves slow growth "at stresses and crack tip stress intensities well below those associated with catastrophic fracture." (p. 1194 [PDF 3]) — HEDGED ("probably").
- Water mechanism (Fig. 2): corrosive, diffusion-controlled attack of water molecules at crack tip hydrolysing siloxane (Si–O–Si) bonds; under load the strained Si–O–Si bonds react faster (p. 1196 [PDF 5], Fig. 2 caption; adopted from refs 5,7,8,31) — DOCUMENTED (literature-based).
- SCG "occurs preferentially in silicate base glasses, which are present in many dental ceramics, and results in bond rupture. Even moisture levels as low as 0.02% relative humidity are known to cause stress corrosion [19,31]." (p. 1198 [PDF 7]) — DOCUMENTED.
- "Slow crack growth involves existing flaws and influences the behavior of especially feldspathic porcelains, glass-ceramics and polycrystalline ceramics." (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- For all ceramics there are stress intensities below which cracks will not grow (threshold K_I0) (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- Clinically, fatigue degradation "is always associated with progressive surface wear (abrasion and attrition)"; wear forms "an extended damage accumulation zone" on the surface, with largest defects progressing to fatigue crack growth (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).

### Microstructure, toughening, polycrystalline ceramics (Lecture §3, §4, §5)
- Transformation-toughened zirconia "and possibly some glass-ceramics" can accumulate internal volume flaws; "distributed microcracking along grain facets and/or interfaces has been documented." (p. 1194 [PDF 3]) — DOCUMENTED (microcracking) / HEDGED (glass-ceramics).
- In non-cubic single-phase ceramics (alumina, zirconia) and composites (glass-ceramics), residual stresses at grain boundaries/phase interfaces develop on cooling and can nucleate microcracking under external stress (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- "In transformation toughened ceramics (e.g., Y-TZP) microcracking occurs during the stress-induced martensitic transformation creating the transformation zone around stressed cracks." (pp. 1194–1196 [PDF 3–5]).
- Smallest flaw size in a partially crystalline material = single microstructural unit (grain/crystallite) (p. 1197 [PDF 6]). Grain size reduction → narrower flaw distribution, lower scatter, "but in contrast they would limit the crack resistance of a material." (p. 1197 [PDF 6]) — stated as principle.
- "The fracture toughness of a ceramic is determined by the size of the microstructural unit and in consequence will determine the slow crack growth resistance" (p. 1197 [PDF 6]).
- In high-crystalline (lithium silicates) or polycrystalline (alumina, zirconia) ceramics, cracks deflect around crystallites/grains; cyclic strength degradation arises from friction between rough opposing crack walls; stress amplitude has stronger influence (low amplitude → little crack opening; high → more friction and degradation) (p. 1197 [PDF 6]) — refs [27].
- Loose debris from deteriorating crack bridges can wedge between crack surfaces and contribute to degradation (p. 1197 [PDF 6]) — ref [28].
- Intrinsic toughening = microstructure ahead of the crack; extrinsic = acts in the wake behind the crack tip (p. 1197 [PDF 6]).
- Lithium disilicate: "Elongated Li2Si2O5 crystals account for an effective crack deflection and twisting of a crack front, thereby substantially dissipating fracture energy." (p. 1197 [PDF 6]); Fig. 5 caption: "microcracking and crack deflection/meandering are effective in leading to a superior fracture toughness among silica based dental ceramics." (p. 1198 [PDF 7]) — DOCUMENTED (SEM shown).
- R-curve effect (increasing resistance to crack growth) "typically found in polycrystalline ceramics (e.g., zirconia) or high vol% crystallized glass-ceramics"; toughening mechanisms responsible for the R-curve "also get degraded and account for the fatigue in these materials." (p. 1198 [PDF 7]). R-curve explicitly outside scope.
- Beneficial effects of cyclic vs static loading may exist when damage involves microcrack deflection or crack-wake bridging "and conceivably also in the case of incremental toughness increases with tetragonal to monoclinic transformation. Such materials are considered 'damage tolerant' [13]." (p. 1198 [PDF 7]) — HEDGED ("conceivably").
- When fatigue primarily involves chemically-assisted crack growth, cycling frequency/waveform "generally have no effect on lifetimes [13]"; when damage involves nucleation of volume defects (intergranular microcracking), higher frequencies "can be more damaging" (p. 1198 [PDF 7]).
- 3Y-TZP double-torsion study: higher SCG susceptibility under constant (static) loading than cyclic; but threshold K_I0 decreased with repetitive cyclic loading [27] (p. 1201 [PDF 10]) — DOCUMENTED.
- Y-TZP cyclic flexure and torsion in air and water: similar crack growth parameters in air and water, but K_I0 lower and crack growth rates higher in water (stress corrosion) [43] (pp. 1200–1201 [PDF 9–10]) — DOCUMENTED.
- Design and processing (powder formation, packing, sintering, machining, finishing) "have a profound influence on the stress distribution during service and the inherent flaw distributions." (p. 1198 [PDF 7]).

### Contact fatigue (Lecture §5, §6)
- Contact fatigue from repetitive tooth–tooth, tooth–restoration, restoration–restoration contact; round indenter on flat → cone crack (begins as surface ring crack, extends into material at an angle) where tensile stresses form [52] (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).
- Ring/cone crack initiation depends mainly on elastic modulus and fracture toughness: "the lower these properties, the lower the necessary load for crack formation [53]." (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).
- Sliding (jaw movement → friction) forms "partial cone cracks, forming a trail behind the moving indenter [53]." (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).

### Clinically relevant failure origins
- Cyclic loading of cemented discs/tabs or flat crowns with blunt piston reproduces radial crack formation from the **intaglio (cementation) surface**, identified as fracture origin in clinically failed crowns (Kelly et al. [48]; Thompson et al. [49]) (p. 1201 [PDF 10]) — DOCUMENTED.
- Multi-unit prostheses: failure most often from cracks originating at the **gingival side of connectors** [43,54] (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).
- Connector "strength" depends on height squared and linearly on width → "connector area is not a good criterion to predict fatigue behavior" (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).

## 7. Controversies / caveats raised

- **Accelerated tests**: valid only "as long as the failure mechanisms (origin, flaw type, damage) under accelerated conditions are similar to those reported for clinical service failures" (p. 1197 [PDF 6]).
- **Frequency**: may be accelerated above clinical 0.5–1 Hz when SCG dominates, but higher frequencies can be more damaging when intergranular microcracking is involved (pp. 1197–1198 [PDF 6–7]).
- **In vitro → clinical**: correlation with long-term clinical outcome "would be an ultimate goal. However, there are several boundary conditions and simplifications regarding the specimen geometry involved in SPT lifetime predictions, restricting the transferability to clinical findings." (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).
- **"Aging" before static test** criticised as "an unfortunate trend"; chewing simulation must be tuned to produce damage, "If not, the investigators are wasting their time and then misleading readers as well." Conditions should be set a priori in a pilot study and not be clinically unrealistic (p. 1202 [PDF 11]).
- **Staircase/step-stress/boundary methods**: efficient but give "a limited picture of the more complete S–N curve"; cannot estimate n and A (p. 1200 [PDF 9]). Staircase gives mean fatigue strength, inaccurate for scatter, ignores run-outs, "For material comparisons only" (Table 1, p. 1195 [PDF 4]).
- **Crack-tracking experiments** use long, less relevant cracks; side crack lengths misrepresent bulk (Table 1, p. 1195 [PDF 4]).
- **Connector area** is not a good predictor; height² matters (p. 1201 [PDF 10]).
- **Out of scope**: R-curve, internal stresses, multilayer/graded components (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).
- Little standardisation for ceramics fatigue: only ISO 14801 (implants) in dentistry; JIS R 1632, ASTM C1368, EN 843-3 for engineering ceramics (p. 1194 [PDF 3]).

## 8. Use in lecture

1. **§5 opener — "ceramics fail below their strength"**: abstract statement (p. 1192) + Fig. 1 concept (flaw grows, strength drops, sudden failure).
2. **§5 — water attacks glass**: Fig. 2 Si–O–Si hydrolysis animation; "even 0.02% RH causes stress corrosion" (p. 1198). Link to §2 feldspathic porcelains (glass-rich → most susceptible).
3. **§5 — three ways to load in fatigue tests**: Fig. 6 (static/dynamic/cyclic); cyclic is "most clinically relevant" (p. 1199).
4. **§5 — S–N curve and endurance limit**: Fig. 9; fatigue limit commonly beyond 10⁵–10⁶ cycles (p. 1200).
5. **§5 — Weibull + time → SPT**: Fig. 10 concept; 98 MPa at 5% failure probability reduced after one year static load (p. 1200).
6. **§3 — why lithium disilicate is tougher**: elongated Li2Si2O5 crystals deflect/twist crack, K_Ic 2–3.5 MPa m^0.5 (p. 1197), Fig. 5 concept.
7. **§4 — transformation toughening**: Fig. 4c (transformation zone around crack); stress-induced martensitic transformation in Y-TZP (pp. 1194–1196); R-curve typical of zirconia (p. 1198); water lowers K_I0 of Y-TZP to ~50% of K_Ic (p. 1201).
8. **§5/§6 — contact damage**: cone cracks from cusp contact; lower modulus and toughness → lower load to crack (p. 1201). Clinical crowns fail from radial cracks at the intaglio surface; bridges from gingival side of connectors (p. 1201).
9. **§5 critical-thinking slide**: why lab fatigue data ≠ clinical survival (p. 1201) and why "aging then load-to-failure" studies may mislead (p. 1202).
10. **§6 summary**: wear and fatigue are coupled clinically (p. 1194).
