## 2. Evidence type and design

- **Type:** meta-narrative literature review that follows the RAMESES publication standard (p.258–259). The authors describe it as a "realistic"/realist approach (p.259, p.268). **No quality assessment** of the included studies was done, which the authors state as a limitation (p.268). On p.268 they also write "the meta-analysis design limits our ability to draw conclusions". That is their wording, but no meta-analysis was performed.
- **Databases:** PubMed, Google Scholar, MEDLINE and CINAHL. Keywords: "zirconia", "lithium disilicate", "antagonist(ic) tooth wear", "enamel wear" (p.258–259). Fig. 1 on p.260 gives the full search strings.
- **Flow (p.259; Fig. 1 p.260):**
  - 1139 records identified (PubMed 234, Google Scholar 750, MEDLINE 123, CINAHL 32).
  - "After removing duplicates (n = 970)". The text does not make clear whether 970 is the number removed or the number remaining.
  - 169 shortlisted; 92 selected after full-text review; 6 added by snowballing; **98 final**.
  - By study type: 67 in vitro, 16 in vivo, 1 combining in vitro and in vivo, 9 systematic reviews, 5 literature reviews.
  - Publication years 2001–2023.
- **Materials covered (p.259):**
  - Enamel antagonists: human n = 57, bovine n = 3.
  - Zirconia: 3YSZ n = 42, 4YSZ n = 3, 5YSZ n = 13, 6YSZ n = 3, strength-gradient n = 1.
  - Lithium disilicate (various compositions) n = 30; zirconia-reinforced lithium disilicate n = 3.
  - Mostly monolithic restorations. One study used lithium disilicate bonded to a zirconia framework and was grouped with lithium disilicate (p.259).
- **Framework:** 12 factors in 4 categories (p.258–259; Fig. 2 p.260):
  1. Mechanical/physical: hardness, surface roughness, fracture toughness, friction coefficient
  2. Wear behaviour and microstructure: wear behaviour, microstructural characteristics, hydrothermal degradation
  3. Surface state: surface finishing procedure
  4. Environment: motion, wear cycling and applied force, geometry, saliva and acidity

## 3. Mechanistic statements (with status)

Key: **CITED-FINDING** = a result the review reports from a cited primary study (the review itself did not test it). **AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION / PROPOSED** = explanation, hypothesis or recommendation. **CONFLICTING** = the review reports evidence in both directions.

### Wear as a system property
- "Wear is not a property of a material but rather a response of a system [3]" (p.258). Framing statement, cited.
- Contact wear can speed up when the restorative material's properties differ from those of hard dental tissue (p.258, [4,5]): higher toughness, higher fracture resistance, altered surface roughness, higher frictional resistance, or higher hardness than teeth.
- Conclusion 1 (p.268): "Wear should not be estimated using a single mechanical parameter, and a conclusion should not be drawn or generalized broadly to all dental ceramics by testing a single type of material." This is the authors' conclusion.

### Hardness does not predict antagonist wear (p.261)
- Hardness has conventionally been used to estimate the wear caused by restorative materials [19]. More wear is "thought to occur" when the antagonist is harder [20]. **Belief reported, not demonstrated.**
- "No relationship between ceramic hardness and the rate of enamel wear in humans has been established [9,17,21]" (p.261). **CITED-FINDING (absence of an established correlation).**
- Hatanaka et al. [12]: wear of bovine enamel was *lower* against a harder material than against a softer one. Lithium disilicate, although softer than zirconia, "can induce more significant enamel wear [22]" (p.261). **CITED-FINDING.**
- There is no correlation between surface hardness and antagonist enamel wear when zirconia is compared with the relatively soft feldspathic porcelain [23] (p.261). **CITED-FINDING.**
- Ceramics wear mainly by fracture rather than plastic deformation, unlike metals [18]. Enamel wear may therefore depend on other mechanical and microstructural features as well as hardness (p.261). **Mechanism cited; the inference is the authors'.**
- Ceramics deform plastically very little, so sharp edges of fractured ceramic are not dulled, and rough asperities may wear enamel as wear continues [18] (p.261). **PROPOSED mechanism, cited.**

### Surface roughness (p.261)
- Roughness also weakens the ceramic and raises the likelihood of chipping [25]. Chips can act as **third-body abrasive particles**. "The degree of enamel wear appears to be well-predicted by the surface roughness of the antagonist [26]" (p.261).
- **Threshold:** "enamel wear is greatly increased when the antagonist surface roughness (Ra) exceeds 1.5 µm [31]". The paper adds that burs used for clinical adjustment produce roughness of this order [31] (p.261). **CITED-FINDING.**
- Mehzabeen [32] and Rodríguez-Rojas [33]: polishing degree had a significant effect on zirconia Ra but **no significant difference in opposing enamel wear** [33]. Roughening polished zirconia did not cause significant antagonist wear "as long as the surface roughness remains well below … Ra = 1.5 µm" [32,33] (p.261). **CITED-FINDING.**
- Polished monolithic zirconia is typically smoother than glazed [7,26,27] and ground [7] zirconia. **CONFLICTING:** Çakmak et al. [28] found glazed zirconia smoother than polished. The authors attribute the inconsistency to different glaze compositions and fabrication methods (p.261).
- Recommendation: regularly repolish roughened occlusal contact areas [34] (p.261).

### Fracture toughness (p.261)
- Low-toughness ceramics may chip in a brittle way during abrasive wear. Chipping can **resharpen particle edges** and raise the wear rate [18] (p.261). **PROPOSED mechanism, cited.**
- Zirconia is more fracture resistant than lithium disilicate, and its crystalline structure "hinders microcrack propagation" [35,36] (p.261).
- The **glaze on zirconia has low fracture toughness**. It is prone to brittle chipping that produces sharp particles acting as abrasive media [26,30] (p.261).
- Sripetchdanond & Leevailoj [36]: microfracture is the predominant ceramic wear mechanism, and toughness is key to preventing it (p.261).
- **Counter-evidence (p.261):**
  - 5YSZ shows less wear than lithium disilicate despite similar toughness [35].
  - Among zirconias, 3YSZ causes the most antagonist enamel wear, then highly translucent 3YSZ, and 5YSZ the least [37].
  - The authors conclude: "fracture toughness alone is not a good predictor of antagonist enamel wear". Note that 3YSZ is the *toughest* zirconia yet wears enamel most in [37].

### Friction coefficient (p.261, p.264)
- The friction coefficient should be matched to that of natural teeth [38]. It depends on surface roughness, contact shape and contact area. Parafunction, higher load and higher sliding velocity raise friction and wear [18] (p.261–264). It also varies with hardness, elastic modulus and surface finish (p.264).
- In lithium disilicate, the **glass matrix wears faster than the crystalline grains** because it is weaker and softer. The resulting roughness raises the maximum friction coefficient [38] (p.264). **CITED-FINDING with mechanistic explanation.**
- Rough lithium disilicate tends to get *smoother* with wear cycling, so friction may fall again over time (p.264).
- Polished zirconia stays smooth over long wear cycling [38–43] and has a stable friction coefficient [38]. The flip side is that rough zirconia keeps its high friction (p.264). **The authors' inference.**
- Few studies have measured tooth/restoration friction (p.264).

### Wear mechanisms and microstructure (p.264)
- **Zirconia:** the predominant mechanism is **polishing wear**, attributed to its high hardness and small grain size [9]. Other possible mechanisms: abrasive wear, fatigue wear [38,45,46], delamination [45]. Subsurface fatigue in enamel causes delamination: cracks nucleate under cyclic chewing load, run parallel to the surface, then turn towards it [45] (p.264).
- **Lithium disilicate:** the principal mechanism is **fatigue wear**. The glassy matrix fractures by fatigue, exposing embedded crystals that are then lost. Composition therefore governs abrasiveness [20] (p.264).
- Zirconia-reinforced lithium disilicate causes significantly more antagonist enamel wear than zirconia [47]. The mechanism is "not well understood" [35,48] (p.264).
- **Microstructure contrast (p.264):** glass-ceramics are crystals in a weak glassy matrix [20]. Zirconia is biphasic tetragonal/cubic with fine grains and strong grain boundaries [56].
- **Transformation toughening is described (p.264):** stress or crack initiation causes a local tetragonal→monoclinic transformation. The volume increase puts compressive stress at the crack tip, which constricts the crack. The paper says this "**may** contribute to the surface integrity and smoothness of zirconia restorations, reducing antagonistic wear [20]". **PROPOSED.**
- Wear depends on yttria content, phase composition and alumina content [37] (p.264).
- High-speed sintering gives smaller grains but "does not necessarily increase antagonist enamel loss [60–62]" (p.264).
- **Lithium disilicate composition (p.264):** novel compositions with more silica, alumina and potassium oxide crystals cause more enamel wear than pressed lithium disilicate. Leucite crystals can detach and roughen the surface [63]. However, crystals can also branch, deflect or dull microcracks and so reduce abrasiveness [54]. Because compositions differ so much, the authors say no general conclusion can be drawn (p.264).
- Ionising radiation (70 Gy) changes the zirconia nanoscale properties through t→m transformation. It does not significantly change microhardness or roughness, and irradiated enamel does not wear more [57,58] (p.264).

### Hydrothermal degradation / LTD (p.265)
- LTD causes surface and subsurface microcracks and surface roughening [64]. Stress, temperature change and moisture make flaws worse and lower strength [65,66].
- Zirconia shows "almost no tetragonal-to-monoclinic phase transformation, even after 10,000 wear cycles in a wet environment" [67] (p.265). **CITED-FINDING.**
- If the transformation stays at the surface, roughness is unchanged. If microcracks grow deep under load and wear, roughness increases [57] (p.265).
- **"Latent effect" model (Badarneh et al. [68]), PROPOSED/HYPOTHESISED (p.265):** degradation effects stay hidden until mechanical loading reveals them. The aged surface layer fractures off instead of being abraded and exposes rough tetragonal zirconia underneath, which means a higher risk of microchipping.
- Glaze may insulate against LTD, but the glaze itself is prone to hydrothermal degradation, microcracking and chipping, so it may increase enamel wear [68] (p.265).
- **CONFLICTING (p.265):**
  - Several studies found that LTD increases enamel wear, but not significantly [23,68,69].
  - Hao et al. [65] found considerably *less* enamel wear against aged zirconia and ascribed it to lower surface hardness. The sentence as printed says this makes it "more prone to induce wear", which is internally contradictory.
  - The sentence on post-wear Ra of aged vs non-aged balls is cited to [57], not [65].
- Madanshetty et al. [37]: 5YSZ does not undergo hydrothermal degradation, unlike 3YSZ and 4YSZ (p.265).
- Clinical note: occlusally adjusted areas will already have undergone t→m transformation [70] (p.265).

### Surface state: polished vs glazed (p.265)
- Antagonist enamel wear is more prevalent against **glazed** zirconia than polished zirconia (p.265, multiple refs [24,26,27,30,31,44,52,67,74–77]).
- The glaze is damaged during wear cycling. Once it is damaged, glazed restorations are rougher than unglazed ones (p.265).
- **Why glaze fails (p.265):**
  - "Inherent flaws within the glaze layer that arise from the entrapment of air during the liquid-mixing process". Stress concentration depends on pre-existing defect size, so chewing cracks the glaze [79]. **PROPOSED mechanism (fracture-mechanics reasoning).**
  - Glaze wear particles act as **third-body abrasives** [26].
  - Glaze wear also occurs on lithium disilicate [80] and exposes the underlying rough surface [63].
- **Counterpoints (p.265):**
  - Glazing can fill and smooth uneven surfaces [4].
  - Glaze in deep grooves is protected by the surrounding zirconia and seals defects [34].
  - Chong et al. [25] found comparable wear for polished vs polished-and-glazed zirconia.
  - Stawarczyk [77]: spray-glazed polished zirconia caused less wear than layer-glazed zirconia after airborne-particle abrasion.
  - The authors conclude that "subsurface conditions may play a role".
- "Under clinical conditions, glaze layers tend to experience wear after approximately **six months**", so the surface may need polishing before glazing [4] (p.265).
- Polished zirconia (dense, void-free polycrystal [30]) causes less antagonist wear than glazed zirconia [27] and than natural enamel [26,30] (p.265). The authors call mechanical polishing "the most effective method for reducing antagonist wear" (p.265). **AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION.**
- **Lithium disilicate (p.265):**
  - Glazing fully crystallised lithium disilicate did not significantly affect antagonist wear [78], in line with [31].
  - Other studies found polished lithium disilicate crowns caused less wear than glazed ones [52,80]. **CONFLICTING.**
  - Chairside polishing of CAD/CAM materials gives roughness equivalent to laboratory polishing [81].
- In vitro, enamel-to-zirconia wear is "generally lower" than enamel-to-enamel wear, regardless of surface preparation [25,30,32,73] (p.265).

### Wear-cycling behaviour of the surface (p.266): key contrast
- Rough-finished lithium disilicate becomes **smoother** with wear cycling [42,98]. Smooth (glazed [35] or polished [42]) lithium disilicate becomes **rougher**.
- "Zirconia surfaces maintain their surface state after wear cycling" (fine grains, strong boundaries [56], hardness and toughness [42], surface features [23]), giving surface integrity [36]. So smooth zirconia stays smooth, but rough zirconia stays rough and is "more likely to cause more abrasive wear" [25,31,39,42] (p.266). **CITED-FINDING plus the authors' mechanistic attribution ("is likely to hinder").**
- **Hypothesis (p.266):** smoothing of lithium disilicate increases contact area and lowers stress on the antagonist. The authors say "more studies are required to confirm this hypothesis". **PROPOSED.**

### Environment (p.266–268)
- **Motion:**
  - Impact dominates early wear (high rate, large loss). Sliding dominates later (steady rate) [95] (p.266).
  - Early impact wear of enamel works by plastic, quasi-plastic and brittle fracture (large fragments, low loss). Later sliding is three-body wear with ploughing and debris and leads to rapid volume loss [95] (p.266).
  - Impact compacts hydroxyapatite, which hardens the worn enamel. A wear-particle layer increases the real contact area, lowers contact stress and absorbs impact energy [96] (p.266).
- **Cycles and load:**
  - Zirconia wear increases with cycle number [34,97]. The opposing enamel's wear *rate* falls over time.
  - Running-in wear dominates after placement; stable wear dominates after about **two years** [93] (p.266).
  - Higher chewing loads raise stress per contact area, so parafunction may change wear behaviour [100] (p.266).
- **Geometry (p.266):**
  - Results depend on specimen geometry [12]. Greater cusp inclination means more wear.
  - Flat cusps allow a wider contact area. Medium/irregular cusp inclinations raise resistance and friction and promote chipping [101].
  - Enamel specimen preparation, morphology and structure also cause discrepancies [102].
  - As cusps flatten, contact area increases and force per unit area falls, so high wear rates "may exhibit **self-limiting** behavior" [93]. The paper advises increasing contact area during occlusal adjustment [95].
- **Saliva:**
  - Saliva lubricates and cools. Without artificial saliva in the lab, wear scars are significantly deeper.
  - Clinically, rapid enamel wear can occur in xerostomia, salivary gland tumours or radiotherapy [103] (p.266–268).
- **Acidity (p.268):**
  - Acid makes zirconia *smoother* with lower friction by **tribocorrosion** [104].
  - Glass-ceramics become more abrasive through etching, crystal deposition and glass corrosion that exposes a rough surface [18].
  - The authors conclude zirconia has the advantage in acidic environments.

### Conclusions (p.268)
1. Wear should not be estimated from a single parameter or generalised from one material.
2. The lithium disilicate glassy matrix has low toughness and poor microstructural characteristics. It is lost during wear cycling, crystals dislodge, roughness rises, and friction and antagonist wear increase.
3. Polished zirconia "may be" a more suitable option in terms of wear.
4. Well-controlled studies are needed on degradation and wear.
5. Behaviour in different oral environments (ageing populations) needs study.

Abstract (p.258): "well-polished zirconia is a more favorable indirect restorative material than lithium disilicate in terms of tribology because of its microstructure and surface integrity during wear."

## 6. Controversies and caveats

- **In vitro vs in vivo disagree.**
  - In vitro: enamel-to-zirconia wear is "generally lower" than enamel-to-enamel wear (p.265).
  - Clinically: "enamel-to-enamel wear is significantly lower than enamel-to-dental ceramic wear, regardless of the surface preparation and material type" (p.266). Also "In vivo, antagonist wear is similar or higher than that against natural teeth when using well-polished monolithic zirconia [93]" (p.266).
  - This is a strong teaching point about the limits of lab tribology.
- **Zirconia vs LD is not unanimous.** "A few studies have reported that the enamel wear rate of zirconia is comparable to [6,12] or greater than that of glass-ceramics [52–55]" (p.264).
- **Glazed vs polished.**
  - Glazed vs polished zirconia roughness conflicts (Çakmak [28], p.261).
  - Glazing fully crystallised LD had no effect [78,31] vs polished LD being better [52,80] (p.265).
  - Spray vs layered glaze technique matters [77] (p.265).
- **LTD effect is unresolved.** Effects were non-significant [23,68,69] or even reduced wear (Hao [65]) (p.265). The authors call for more studies.
- **Method and quality.** No quality assessment (p.268). Test geometry [12,101,102], motion type [95], saliva [103] and cycles all change results (p.266). Composition of "lithium disilicate" varies widely (p.259, p.264).
- **Internal inconsistencies to avoid quoting.**
  - 40.6 µm (text p.266) vs 40.06 µm (Table 3 p.267).
  - "meta-analysis design" (p.268), although the review is a meta-narrative.
  - The Hao [65] hardness sentence is self-contradictory (p.265).
  - The Table 2 value-to-row mapping is ambiguous.
- Hardness *in vitro*: Hatanaka [12] used **bovine** enamel (p.261). Human enamel is preferred for extrapolation.

## 7. Use in lecture

- **Section 6, opening line:** "Wear is not a property of a material but rather a response of a system" (Kyaw 2024, p.258). Pair it with an original redraw of the Fig. 2 four-category map (p.260).
- **Myth-buster: "harder = more enamel wear".**
  - No established relationship between ceramic hardness and enamel wear rate in humans (p.261).
  - LD is softer than zirconia but can cause more wear (p.261).
  - Ceramics wear by fracture, not plastic deformation (p.261).
  - Good for a misconception box and a quiz item.
- **Toughness is not enough either.** 3YSZ (toughest) wears enamel most, 5YSZ least [37], and 5YSZ beats LD at similar toughness [35] (p.261). Use this to show that a single property never predicts a system response.
- **Mechanism animation idea (two-panel).**
  - (a) LD: the glassy matrix wears and fatigues away, crystals pull out, roughness and friction rise (p.264).
  - (b) Polycrystalline zirconia: fine grains and strong boundaries, the polished surface stays smooth ("polishing wear"), so friction is stable (p.264, p.266).
  - Caveat: rough zirconia also stays rough (p.266).
- **Glaze life-cycle schematic.**
  - Glaze with entrapped air bubbles → chewing cracks it at flaws → chips become third-body abrasives → after about 6 months the substrate is exposed (p.265).
  - Supports the take-home "polish the occlusal contact, glaze is not a wear shield".
- **Roughness threshold:** Ra > 1.5 µm greatly increases enamel wear, and clinical adjustment burs produce roughness of that order, hence repolish after adjustment (p.261, p.265). Clinical-reasoning checkpoint.
- **Data redraw:** Tachibana [42] polished/ground zirconia vs LD Ra before and after wear (Table 2, p.263). Shows that surface state evolves differently by microstructure.
- **Environment slide:**
  - Saliva lubricates (p.266–268).
  - Acid: zirconia tribocorrosion makes it smoother, glass-ceramic etching makes it rougher (p.268).
  - Impact early vs sliding late (p.266).
  - Cusp inclination and contact area; self-limiting wear (p.266).
- **Caveat box: lab vs mouth.** In vitro, enamel vs zirconia < enamel vs enamel. In vivo the reverse (p.265–266). Ideal for the "critically appraise manufacturer claims" outcome.
- Clinical wear magnitudes (µm per year, Table 3) can be *mentioned* as the order of magnitude only. Do not present them as survival or clinical-performance teaching content.
