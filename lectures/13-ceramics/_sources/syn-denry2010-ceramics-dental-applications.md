## 3. Key mechanistic statements by lecture section

D = **DEMONSTRATED/reported** in cited work ("has been shown/reported", "revealed"). P = **PROPOSED** ("likely", "potentially", "suggested", "expected", "proposed").

### Section 1 — Introduction / classification / basic properties
- Ceramics are in demand in dentistry "due to their chemical inertness and a wide combination of optical properties" (PDF p.1 [351]).
- Classification is by **crystalline phase and fabrication technique** (Table 1, PDF p.2 [352]). The authors say this classification "is constantly evolving" (p.1).
- All-ceramics contain "from about 35 to about 99 vol %" crystalline phase. Higher crystallinity improves mechanical properties "through various mechanisms, such as crystalline reinforcement or stress-induced transformation" (PDF p.4 [354]).
- "Higher crystallinity is also associated with higher opacity". 3Y-TZP is "the most opaque of all-ceramic materials currently available" [19] (PDF p.4).
- Other determinants of mechanical response: "crystal size and geometry, modulus of elasticity, phase transformation and thermal expansion mismatch between crystal and glassy phase" (PDF p.4).
- Conclusions: the proportions of glassy phase and porosity "both influence optical and mechanical properties". Residual stresses between crystalline phases and glassy matrix, and microcracking, "play a key role" (PDF p.12 [362]).
- Amorphous vs crystalline structure at atomic level, and ionic/covalent bonding: **not stated**. The paper does not define bonding.

### Section 2 — Feldspathic porcelains
- Veneering ceramics for metal-ceramics ("feldspathic porcelains") "are usually leucite-based" (PDF p.2 [352]).
- Feldspar glass alone has a low CTE, ~8.6 × 10⁻⁶/K. **Adding leucite raises the CTE** to match the metal (PDF p.2). D
- Leucite (KAlSi₂O₆) is tetragonal at room temperature. It undergoes a **displacive tetragonal→cubic transformation at 625 °C with +1.2 % volume expansion**, which gives a high CTE of 20–25 × 10⁻⁶/K. In porcelains the transformation can start "as low as 400 °C" (PDF p.2). D
- Leucite is obtained by incongruent melting of feldspar at 1150–1530 °C. Varying the leucite:glass ratio lets the manufacturer "precisely adjust" the CTE (PDF p.2).
- Lamellar twinning in leucite is "due to the phase transformation, so as to minimize macroscopic strain" (Fig. 1) (PDF p.2).
- Leucite content is 15–25 vol %. It is adjusted so the porcelain's thermal contraction is **slightly lower than that of the metal**, which places the ceramic under slight compression (PDF p.2). D (design principle)
- Sintering under vacuum reduces porosity. Pores, cracks and inclusions are also present (Fig. 2) (PDF p.2).
- CTE mismatch between leucite and glass produces "radial tensile stresses and tangential compressive stresses ... within and around the crystals upon cooling, leading to the formation of microcracks" (PDF p.3 [353]). D (cited [10])
- Decoupling of leucite from the matrix "has been reported" and may affect CTE. Multiple firings and slow cooling "have also been shown" to change leucite content and therefore expansion behaviour (PDF p.3). D
- Mechanical properties of feldspathic porcelains are "the lowest of ceramic materials used in dentistry and dominated by the large amount of glassy phase" (PDF p.3).
- Clinical context: >50 % of all restorations in 2005 were estimated to be metal-ceramic [18] (PDF p.3).
- Veneered zirconia: crazing or cracking at the veneer/core interface "reported clinically" [68–72]. "Mechanisms involving destabilization of the tetragonal phase at the interface with the veneering porcelain have been proposed" [73] (PDF p.11 [361]). P
- Chipping of veneering ceramic is a complication in 3Y-TZP FPDs [77] (PDF p.11).
- **HF etching + silane: not stated anywhere in this paper.**
- Residual stress from veneer/zirconia CTE matching: **not stated** specifically. Only the metal-ceramic CTE principle is given.

### Section 2b — Leucite-reinforced heat-pressed (bridge to glass-ceramics)
- First-generation heat-pressed ceramics have 35–45 vol % leucite. Flexural strength and K_Ic are "about two times higher" than feldspathic porcelain [26]. This is attributed to dispersion of fine leucite crystals [27] (PDF p.5 [355]).
- Tangential compressive stresses around crystals "can contribute to crack deflection" [28]. P/D
- "Coalescence of microcracks can also cause decoupling ... and lead to a degradation in strength and fracture toughness" [29] (PDF p.5).
- About 9 % porosity [27]. Strength increases after additional firings because of further leucite crystallisation [30]. Tetragonal leucite is the stable phase under lab conditions [31] (PDF p.5).

### Section 3 — Lithium disilicate glass-ceramics
- Heat-pressed (second generation): "about 65 vol % lithium disilicate ... with about 1% porosity" [27] (PDF p.5 [355]).
- Crystallisation mechanisms are "somewhat complex, due to the presence of nanosized crystal phases" [32] (PDF p.5).
- High-temperature XRD: **lithium metasilicate (Li₂SiO₃) and cristobalite (SiO₂) form before lithium disilicate (Li₂Si₂O₅) grows** [37] (PDF p.5). D
- Final microstructure: "highly interlocked lithium disilicate crystals, 5 μm in length, 0.8 μm in diameter" (Fig. 4) (PDF p.5).
- CTE mismatch between crystals and glass is "likely to result in tangential compressive stresses around the crystals, potentially responsible for crack deflection and strength increase" (Borom [32]) (PDF p.5). **P**
- Interlocked microstructure and layered crystals "are also likely to contribute to strengthening" (Fig. 5) (PDF p.5). **P**
- "Crack propagation is easy along the cleavage planes, but more difficult across the planes, leading to multiple crack deflections due to an array of crystal orientations" (PDF p.5). Stated as mechanism.
- Crystal alignment after heat-pressing has been "reported" [27,38,39]. It is "likely to affect differently the mechanical properties", with higher crack resistance perpendicular to alignment (PDF p.5). D (alignment) / P (anisotropy)
- LD strength is "more than twice that of first generation leucite-reinforced all-ceramics" (PDF p.6 [356]).
- **Machinable (CAD/CAM) LD (two-stage):**
  - Blocks are partially crystallised, with lithium metasilicate crystals plus LD nuclei. They are "easy to machine" at 130 MPa (manufacturer's data) (PDF p.9 [359]).
  - HT blocks have fewer, larger metasilicate crystals. LT blocks have a higher density of smaller crystals (Fig. 9a, 9c).
  - Full crystallisation at **850 °C for 10 min**:
    - HT: layered LD crystals 1.5 × 0.8 μm.
    - LT: "high density of small (0.8 × 0.2 μm) interlocked" LD crystals.
    - Spherical pores in both are "interpreted as" highly soluble lithium phosphate crystals. "X-ray diffraction data (not presented)" (PDF p.9). P (interpretation; data not shown)
  - Strength after crystallisation: 360 MPa (manufacturer). One study gives 134 ± 27 MPa as received and 262 ± 88 MPa after crystallisation (3-point bending) [55] (PDF p.9).
- Crack bridging in LD: **not stated** (bridging is mentioned only for alumina, p.7–8).
- Optics/translucency of LD: only HT vs LT pre-crystal density (p.9). Mechanism linking crystal size to translucency: **not stated**.
- Wear of LD: **not stated**.
- HF etching of LD: **not stated**.

### Section 4 — Polycrystalline (alumina, zirconia)
**Alumina (dry-pressed and sintered):**
- Enlarged die compensates for 12–20 % sintering shrinkage. Sintered at 1550 °C.
- Mean grain size ~4 μm. Flexural strength 601 ± 73 MPa [40–42] (PDF p.6 [356]).
- Clinical performance described as "excellent ... at 15 years" [43] (PDF p.7 [357]).

**Glass-infiltrated alumina (slip-cast):**
- Composition 68 vol % alumina, 27 vol % glass, 5 vol % porosity.
- "Evidence of grain pull-out, bridging and crack deflection was reported" [27] (PDF p.7). D
- CTE mismatch "could contribute to strengthening" (suggested). P
- Large high-RI alumina crystals plus porosity give opacity. Spinel gives better translucency at lower strength (PDF p.7).

**ZTA slip-cast:**
- Composition 34 vol % alumina, 33 vol % 12Ce-TZP, ~23 vol % glass, ~8 vol % porosity (PDF p.7).
- Two strengthening mechanisms (PDF p.7–8 [357–358]):
  - (1) Stress-induced transformation produces compressive stresses in transformed grains plus circumferential tensile stresses, with microcrack nucleation. "Transgranular fracture is difficult in zirconia".
  - (2) "Crack deflection, contact shielding and crack bridging are expected" from large alumina grains. P

**Zirconia (3Y-TZP, soft-machined):**
- Soft machining proposed in 2001. Design compensates **~25 % volume shrinkage** on sintering (PDF p.10 [360]).
- "Unalloyed zirconia is monoclinic at room temperature and tetragonal above 1170 °C" [59] (PDF p.10).
- **t→m transformation: "substantial volume increase (~4.5%)"** (PDF p.10).
- Tetragonal phase can be stabilised at room temperature by yttria, ceria, calcia or magnesia (PDF p.10).
- **Transformation toughening:** "transformation from tetragonal to monoclinic phase at the crack tip, associated with a volume increase, thereby creating compressive stresses", "efficient in preventing further crack propagation" (PDF p.10–11). D (established, cited [61–63])
- Stability "strongly depend[s] on its grain size". Above a critical grain size 3Y-TZP is "less stable and more susceptible to spontaneous transformation". Smaller grains have a lower transformation rate (PDF p.11 [361]). D
- Grain size depends on sintering temperature and time. Higher temperature or longer time gives larger grains (Fig. 10). Commercial sintering ranges 1350–1550 °C for 2–6 h (PDF p.11).
- **Cubic phase and its temperature: not stated.**
- **4Y/5Y: not stated.**
- **Translucency/birefringence of zirconia: not stated**, beyond "most opaque" (p.4).
- **MDP adhesion: not stated.**

**Low-temperature degradation (LTD):**
- 3Y-TZP "undergo[es] microstructural degradation in a humid environment at relatively low temperatures" [21–23] (PDF p.4).
- LTD involves "grain pull-out, microcracking and surface roughening" [82,85,86] (PDF p.12 [362]).
- ISO standard for Y-TZP implants: **monoclinic phase ≤25 % after accelerated ageing for 5 h (XRD)** [87 = ISO 13356:2008] (PDF p.12).
- Surface finish and residual surface stresses "strongly influence" LTD [88]. Significant roughening can occur even with <25 % monoclinic after 5 h (PDF p.12). D

**ZTA/ATZ composites:**
- "Less susceptible to low temperature degradation".
- Ce-TZP/alumina nanocomposites: 1422 ± 60 MPa with "excellent resistance to LTD" [92,93] (PDF p.12).

**Clinical (3Y-TZP FPDs):** "excellent success rate but a lower survival rate due to complications such as secondary caries and chipping of the veneering ceramic" [77] (PDF p.11).

### Section 5 — Fatigue
- "A humid environment is susceptible to lead to stress corrosion and catastrophic failure in ceramic materials including a glassy phase" [20] (PDF p.4 [354]).
- The oral environment includes "humidity, acidic or basic pH, cyclic loading and peak loads" (PDF p.4).
- "It is therefore generally accepted that tests performed in a humid environment and under cyclic loading are needed" [24] (PDF p.5 [355]).
- Machining fully sintered ceramics creates "residual surface flaws that could, in the long-term, be detrimental" [54] (PDF p.9).
- **Slow crack growth parameters, Weibull statistics, S–N curves: not stated.**

### Section 6 — Ceramics vs resin composites
- **Not stated.** Resin composites are not discussed. Wear and crystallinity–optics comparisons with composites are absent.

## 6. Controversies and caveats; claims without primary support

1. **"Ten-fold increase in flexural strength and fracture toughness"** (p.12). No K_Ic values appear anywhere in the paper. The toughness half of the claim is unsupported within this text. Strength runs from 61 MPa (Table 2, porcelain) to 1087 MPa (3Y-TZP), which is roughly an order of magnitude. Present this as approximate.
2. **Alumina strength inconsistency:** 601 ± 73 MPa in the text (p.6) vs 607 ± 73 MPa in Table 2 (p.13).
3. **Table 2 mixes test methods and sources.** Most values are single studies, with test geometry not given in the table. Three values are **manufacturer's data** (130, 360 MPa LD; 700 MPa In-Ceram AL). The IPS Empress CAD row reuses the pressed-leucite value (106 ± 17, [27]), justified only by "similar in microstructure" (p.9). Ref [55] (262 ± 88 MPa) is cited as "J. Dent. Res. 2007, 88, 224", a single-page citation that appears to be a conference abstract. Verify before quoting.
4. **LD strengthening mechanisms are hedged** ("likely", "potentially": residual tangential compression, crack deflection, anisotropy). They are PROPOSED, not demonstrated, in this paper.
5. **Fig. 9 pore interpretation:** "interpreted as lithium phosphate crystals". XRD was used "to confirm" but is "not presented".
6. **ZTA mechanisms** "are expected" (p.8). This is inference, not shown.

## 7. Use in lecture

- **§2 Feldspathic:** Use Fig. 1 (leucite twinning) plus Fig. 3a (leucite with microcracks in glass) to show the leucite story in two steps:
  1. t→c at 625 °C, +1.2 % volume, high CTE 20–25 × 10⁻⁶/K vs glass ~8.6 × 10⁻⁶/K (p.2)
  2. Then the mismatch microcracks (p.3)
  - Avoid Fig. 3b (third-party).
- **CTE matching rule (p.2):** porcelain contraction is "slightly lower than that of the metal" to put the veneer in compression. Use this as a structure → property → performance example.
- **§3 LD:** Fig. 4 (interlocked rods) and Fig. 5a (layered crystals). Teach "cleavage-plane crack deflection", and say the residual-stress explanation is proposed, not proven.
- **§3 CAD/CAM two-stage LD:** Fig. 9 as a 4-panel build. Metasilicate (machinable, 130 MPa manufacturer) → 850 °C/10 min → disilicate. Then HT vs LT crystal density.
- **§1 Crystallinity–optics trade-off (p.4):** 35–99 vol % crystalline; higher crystallinity → stronger but more opaque; 3Y-TZP most opaque. Pair with a slider interaction.
- **§4 Zirconia:**
  - 1170 °C m→t; t→m +4.5 %; transformation toughening at the crack tip (p.10–11)
  - Fig. 10 (1300 vs 1500 °C) to show grain size ↔ stability
  - ISO 13356 ≤25 % monoclinic after 5 h ageing (p.12) as the LTD benchmark

- **§5 Fatigue framing (p.4–5):** stress corrosion in humid environment; "tests ... in a humid environment and under cyclic loading are needed". Fig. 2 (pores, cracks, inclusions) introduces the flaw population that drives fatigue.
