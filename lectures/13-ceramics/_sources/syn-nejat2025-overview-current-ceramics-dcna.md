## 3. Key mechanistic statements by lecture section

**DEMONSTRATED** = backed by cited primary data/SR in this text; **PROPOSED** = interpretation; **[NR]** = narrative assertion, cited to secondary/review literature or uncited.

### Section 1 — Introduction / classification
- Ceramic advantages: optical, mechanical (fracture/wear resistance), biological (biocompatibility, inertness), chemical stability, thermal insulation (p. 155). [NR]
- **Classification by composition:** "glass-matrix based, polycrystalline, and resin-matrix based" (p. 156, refs 1, 3). Purpose: understand pros/cons and recognise the optimal surface treatment for resin cementation (p. 156).
- **Classification by manufacturing:** layering, slip-casting, heat-pressing, subtractive CAD/CAM (milling), additive CAD/CAM (3D printing) (p. 156).
- Glass-based ceramics = amorphous glass phase + crystalline phase; glass (mainly SiO2) → high translucency, relatively low fracture resistance, brittleness; **"The glass phase plays an important role in the process of bonding the resin cements to ceramics"**; crystalline phase reinforces and controls optical properties (p. 156). [NR]
- Polycrystalline ceramics: no glassy phase, densely arranged grains → higher mechanical properties; absence of glass + anisotropic grains → lower translucency (p. 165). [NR]

### Section 2 — Feldspathic porcelains / leucite
- Feldspar-reinforced glass-ceramic: feldspar (potassium KAlSi3O8 and/or sodium NaAlSi3O8), quartz/silica (SiO2), kaolin (Al2O3·2SiO2·2H2O) (p. 156).
- "Kaolin is opaque and is added to improve the mechanical properties" (p. 156). [NR — see Controversies]
- Uses: veneering metal or ceramic cores, or monolithic veneer on platinum foil or refractory die (p. 156).
- Feldspathic porcelain contains **15–25 vol% crystalline phase (leucite)**; fine-particle feldspar CAD/CAM blocks ~30 vol% crystalline (p. 156).
- Layering with feldspathic porcelain increases risk of fracture and chipping vs monolithic (p. 156). [cited]
- **Leucite (KAlSi2O6) originates after heating feldspar to ~1300 °C** (p. 156). [NR]
- Leucite "plays an important role in reinforcing the ceramic through toughening mechanisms that inhibit crack propagation" (p. 156). [NR — mechanism not named]
- **Leucite refractive index is close to that of the glass matrix → relatively high translucency** (p. 156, refs 14–16). [NR]
- Leucite content: pressable 40–50 wt%; CAD/CAM blocks 35–45 vol% (p. 156). (Note mixed units as printed.)
- Third-generation zirconia aims to eliminate incisal layering with feldspathic porcelain "as the layering can create a weak point in the restoration and lead to chipping and fracture" (p. 166). [NR]
- CTE matching of porcelain to metal or zirconia: **not stated**. HF etch: only manufacturer protocols in Table 1 (5% HF 60 s for VITA Mark II and IPS Empress CAD, p. 157).

### Section 3 — Lithium disilicate and related glass-ceramics
- LDS = LDS crystals **65–70 vol%** in glass matrix; two major crystal phases: lithium metasilicate (Li2SiO3) and lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5) (p. 156).
- CAD/CAM blocks contain lithium metasilicate and P2O5; precrystallised state flexural strength **≈130 MPa** → easier machining (p. 164).
- Above **750 °C**, P2O5 acts as nucleating agent promoting volume nucleation and crystallisation of lithium metasilicate into LDS → "high volume of well-interdigitated and long LDS crystals with a small amount of residual glass matrix and lithium orthophosphate (Li3PO4)" → biaxial flexural strength >400 MPa (p. 164). [NR, cited to review refs]
- **CTE mismatch mechanism:** "the mismatch between the coefficients of thermal expansion of LDS crystals and that of the glass-matrix results in a compressive stress around the crystals, which increases the fracture resistance of LDS ceramics" (p. 164, refs 10, 22). PROPOSED/NR — no CTE values, no direction of mismatch stated.
- Crack deflection / bridging by name: **not stated**. ("well-interdigitated" is the only microstructural toughening descriptor.)
- Pressable LDS ingots also available fully crystallised (p. 164).
- Clinical: high survival as single-tooth restorations; caution for FPDs ("low survival rates and irreparable complications") (p. 164, SR ref 24).
- Post-patent LDS innovations (p. 164): fully crystallised LDS block (GC Initial LiSi); fully crystallised lithium aluminosilicate block reinforced with LDS (n!ce) requiring no firing; LDS in glassy matrix with lithium aluminosilicate crystals Li0.5Al0.5Si2.5O6 ("virgilite") requiring only one glaze firing (CEREC Tessera); nano-sized LDS precrystallised block (Amber Mill) with translucency adjustable by firing cycle.
- **Lithium silicate (LS)** (p. 164): dendritic Li2SiO3 nanocrystals → spheroidise by solution–reprecipitation → round LS crystals **43 vol%** + new Li3PO4 nanocrystals. Limited clinical data; at 1 y lower opposing-enamel wear and comparable success vs monolithic zirconia (refs 27, 28).
- **ZLS** (pp. 164–165): round submicrometric Li2SiO3 and Li3PO4 crystals; **tetragonal zirconia particles (10 wt%)** added to the glass matrix; crystallisation firing converts smaller Li2SiO3 into LDS and "removes the inherent cracks inside the glass-matrix" → higher flexural strength, hardness, fracture resistance; fully crystallised material biphasic (LS + LDS) with zirconia dispersed in glass. ZLS has more glass matrix (**45 vol%**) than LDS; "one of the most difficult glass-ceramics to machine"; **higher translucency than LDS → lower masking ability**; "no conclusive evidence of higher mechanical strength for ZLS compared to LDS" (p. 165).
- Bonding (p. 156 + Table 1): glass phase enables resin-cement bonding; HF etch times per manufacturer (Table 1). **Silane for glass-ceramics: not stated** (silane only mentioned for Lava Ultimate fillers, p. 167).

### Section 4 — Polycrystalline ceramics (alumina, zirconia)
- Alumina and zirconia are the primary polycrystalline dental ceramics; zirconia predominates (p. 165). Alumina properties: **not stated**.
- **Phases** (p. 165): monoclinic — stable; grains "approximately 4% to 5% larger than tetragonal grains"; tetragonal — mechanically strong, transforms to monoclinic "in the presence of stress, such as cracks or exposure to a moist environment"; cubic — lowest mechanical properties, highest optical properties, isotropic (refractive index independent of orientation), no transformation capability.
- **Transformation toughening** (p. 165): t→m transformation "results in larger monoclinic grains that can prevent further crack propagation". [NR — simplified; no mention of compressive stress zone]
- Tetragonal phase is anisotropic → light deflection at grain boundaries → lower translucency (p. 165).
- More cubic → more translucency, lower mechanical properties (p. 165).
- **Generations** (pp. 165–166): 1st gen 3 mol% yttria (3Y), predominantly tetragonal, monochromatic, strong but opaque. 2nd gen polychromatic discs with varying yttria: **3Y highest tetragonal; 4Y ≈75% tetragonal / 25% cubic; 5Y ≈50% tetragonal / 50% cubic**. 3rd gen: translucency (yttria) gradient — more yttria incisally (more cubic, more translucent), less cervically (more tetragonal).
- **LTD:** only in AM context — additively manufactured zirconia shows "a higher rate of phase transformation and low temperature degradation" vs milled (p. 166, ref 51). LTD mechanism otherwise not described (moist environment mentioned as a t→m trigger, p. 165).
- AM zirconia vs milled (p. 166): higher flexural strength; comparable fit/accuracy; similar grain size and crystal structure; comparable fracture toughness; higher LTD; lower surface hardness; more roughness and porosity. Open issues: powder-to-liquid ratio, slurry viscosity/air bubbles, agglomerates, interlayer defects, nesting angle, supports, debinding, sintering. Technologies: vat polymerisation (DLP, SLA) and 3D gel deposition (p. 166).
- **Zirconia surface treatment (Table 1, manufacturer):** air-particle abrasion with alumina (e.g., 50 µm at 1–3 bar, 10 s; or 25–70 µm at 1 bar / 70–110 µm at 1.5 bar) (pp. 160–161). **MDP primers: not stated.**
- Yttria mol% for 4Y and 5Y explicitly: implied by names "4 mol% yttria (4Y)", "5 mol% yttria (5Y)" (p. 165). Grain size numbers, t→m volume change (other than "4%–5% larger grains"), transformation temperatures: **not stated**.

### Section 5 — Fatigue

### Section 6 — Resin-matrix ceramics / ceramics vs composites
- Resin-matrix ceramics: organic matrix with high ratio of ceramic filler (p. 166). ADA CDT 2013: "predominantly inorganic refractory compounds" replaced "nonresin inorganic refractory compounds" (p. 166). 2023: ADA removed "pressed, fired, polished, or milled" requirement; 3D-printed materials with >50% filler accepted as permanent ceramic crowns (p. 167).
- "In comparison to all-ceramic materials, resin-matrix materials are not brittle and have shock-absorbing properties", relevant to implants lacking PDL damping (p. 166, ref 59). [NR — overstatement, see Controversies]
- Lava Ultimate ("resin nanoceramic"): silica + zirconia nanoparticles/nanoclusters 80 wt% in highly cross-linked polymer; silane-treated fillers; E 14.5 GPa (p. 167).
- VITA Enamic (PICN): ceramic network feldspar 86 wt% / 75 vol%; polymer methacrylate 14 wt% / 25 vol%; E 28 GPa "between that of dentin and enamel" (p. 167).
- CERASMART ("flexible nanoceramic"): alumina-barium-silicate in polymer; higher flexural strength, E 10 GPa (p. 167).
- AVENCIA: compressed nanosilica 62 wt% impregnated with monomer, heat-polymerised; E 8 GPa; higher polishability (p. 167).
- Printable "permanent" resins: up to 50% filler (silica-based in some), methacrylate matrix (UDMA, Bis-GMA; TEGDMA, Bis-EMA as diluents); lower filler needed for flow and light transmission (p. 167).
- Clinical: Lava Ultimate debonding → contraindicated for full coverage; 80% debonding within first year on implant (zirconia) abutments (p. 167, refs 65, 66). Others: acceptable short-term performance (p. 167).
- Wear: LS lower opposing-enamel wear than monolithic zirconia at 1 y (p. 164). Optics vs crystallinity: glass → translucency; anisotropic tetragonal grains → scattering; cubic → isotropic (pp. 156, 165).

## 5. Classification schemes (verbatim)

- By composition (p. 156): "glass-matrix based, polycrystalline, and resin-matrix based". Table 1 categories: **"Glass-matrix"**, **"Polycrystalline"**, **"Resin-matrix"** (pp. 157–162).
- Glass-matrix types (Table 1): "Feldspar-reinforced", "Leucite-reinforced", "Lithium Disilicate", "Lithium Silicate", "Zirconia-reinforced Lithium Silicate" (pp. 157–159). Text names: "Feldspar-reinforced glass-ceramic", "Leucite-reinforced glass-ceramic", "Lithium disilicate glass-ceramic (LDS)", "Lithium silicate glass-ceramic (LS)", "Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate glass-ceramics (ZLS)" (pp. 156, 164).
- Polycrystalline types (Table 1): "3Y", "4Y", "5Y", "Composition-gradient" (pp. 160–161); text: first/second/third "generation" zirconia (pp. 165–166).
- Resin-matrix types (Table 1): "Subtractively Manufactured (Milled)", "Additively Manufactured (Printed)" (pp. 161–162); text names "resin nanoceramic", "polymer-infiltrated ceramic network", "flexible nanoceramic" (p. 167).
- By manufacturing (p. 156): "layering, slip-casting, heat-pressing, subtractive computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing ([CAD/CAM], also called milling), and additive CAD/CAM (also called 3D printing)".
- Note: the lecture's "glassy vs glass-ceramic vs polycrystalline" scheme is **not** used here; this paper's three classes are glass-matrix / polycrystalline / resin-matrix (no separate "predominantly glassy" class; feldspathic is grouped under glass-matrix as "feldspar-reinforced glass-ceramic").

## 7. Controversies / caveats raised

- Glass-based ceramics need cautious consideration for FPDs (low survival, irreparable complications) (p. 164).
- Very limited clinical data for LS (p. 164) and ZLS (p. 165); no conclusive evidence ZLS stronger than LDS (p. 165).
- AM zirconia: many unresolved processing issues (p. 166).
- Resin-matrix ceramics: limited clinical data; Lava Ultimate debonding; difficulty running long-term trials given rapid product turnover (p. 167).
- Table 1 strength values not comparable across products (p. 162 footnote a).
- **Reviewer flags (mine):**
  1. "Kaolin is opaque and is added to improve the mechanical properties" (p. 156) — cited only to reviews (refs 8, 9); verify against a primary dental-porcelain source before teaching.
  2. Leucite "toughening mechanisms" are not named (p. 156); the CTE-mismatch compressive-stress argument is applied in this paper to **LDS** (p. 164), not to leucite. Do not transfer it without a source.
  3. Transformation toughening explained as "larger monoclinic grains prevent crack propagation" (p. 165) — oversimplified; Silva 2017 (p. 134) gives the compressive-stress-around-defects explanation. Teach the stress version.
  4. "Tetragonal transforms … in the presence of … exposure to a moist environment" (p. 165) conflates toughening with ageing (LTD); separate these on slides.
  5. ZLS zirconia described as "tetragonal zirconia particles (10 wt%) … dispersed in the glass-matrix" (pp. 164–165) vs Zhang 2023's "zirconosilicate glass" (JDR p. 247). Unresolved between sources.
  6. "Resin-matrix materials are not brittle and have shock-absorbing properties" (p. 166) is an overstatement (supported by one damping study, ref 59); resin composites still fracture in a brittle manner — flag.
  7. LDS crystal content 65–70 vol% (p. 156) is consistent with Zhang 2023 (~70 vol%, p. 247); LS 43 vol% matches Zhang 2023 Obsidian (p. 247).
  8. Leucite content mixes wt% (pressable) and vol% (CAD) (p. 156) — not directly comparable.

## 8. Use in lecture

- Opening classification slide: glass-matrix / polycrystalline / resin-matrix (p. 156) — and explain why classification predicts the bonding route (glass phase → HF etching; no glass → air-particle abrasion) (p. 156; Table 1 pp. 157–162).
- Feldspathic composition slide: K/Na feldspar + quartz + kaolin; 15–25 vol% leucite; leucite forms ~1300 °C; refractive-index match → translucency (p. 156).
- LDS slide: blue block (Li2SiO3, ≈130 MPa, machinable) → >750 °C → 65–70 vol% interdigitated LDS (>400 MPa) (pp. 156, 164); present the CTE-mismatch compressive-stress idea as a proposed mechanism.
- Zirconia phase slide: m/t/c properties; 3Y vs 4Y (~75 t / 25 c) vs 5Y (~50 / 50) → translucency up, strength down; cubic cannot transformation-toughen (p. 165). Pair with manufacturer flexural strengths 1150–1200 (3Y) / 750–850 (4Y) / 550 (5Y) clearly labelled as manufacturer data (pp. 160–161).
- Gradient (3rd generation) zirconia to eliminate veneering porcelain and its chipping (pp. 165–166).
- "Brand vs measurement" critical-appraisal moment: Tessera 700 MPa (manufacturer, Table 1 p. 158) vs 367 MPa measured (Zhang 2023 p. 246).
- Ceramics vs composites summary: resin-matrix "ceramics" (ADA definitions 2013/2023, p. 166–167), E values 8–28 GPa vs glass-ceramics; Lava Ultimate debonding lesson (p. 167).
- Clinical anchors: monolithic zirconia 91–100% survival; 0% vs 7.61% chipping vs metal-ceramic on implants (p. 166); ZLS molar partial crowns 69% success at 5 y with thin occlusal sections failing (p. 165) → thickness matters.
- Do not use for fatigue mechanisms, CTE values, LTD mechanism, MDP, or K_Ic — not covered.
