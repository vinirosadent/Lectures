## 3. Key mechanistic statements by lecture section

Labels: **DEMONSTRATED** = supported by data shown in this paper or a cited primary study; **PROPOSED** = authors' interpretation/hypothesis; **[NR]** = narrative assertion without primary data in this paper.

### Section 1 — Introduction / classification
- Glass–ceramics are "inherently brittle but can be manufactured with acceptably high strengths by compositional and processing refinements" (p. 245). [NR]
- "Ceramics do not approach the toughness of metals, and fractures can propagate from any defect in a ceramic prosthesis, exacerbated by moisture-assisted crack growth and mechanical fatigue in cyclic loading" (p. 245, cites Zhang et al. 2013). [NR, cited]
- Bite forces "can exceed several hundred newtons" (p. 245). [NR]
- History: first synthetic glass–ceramic by S.D. Stookey (Corning) 1954, a partially crystallised lithium disilicate glass (p. 245). DICOR (Corning) = first commercial dental glass–ceramic: tetrasilicic fluorine mica crystals as sheets/flakes in glass, "house of cards" microstructure, easy cleavage at crystal–glass interfaces enabling machining; withdrawn because of high fracture rate (p. 246). IPS Empress (leucite aluminosilicate) introduced 1991; IPS Empress 2 (1998) first commercial lithia-based silicate glass–ceramic; IPS e.max Press 2005, e.max CAD 2006 (p. 246).

### Section 2 — Feldspathic / leucite
- Only historical mention: IPS Empress is "a leucite aluminosilicate material" (1991), CAD and Press versions still in use (p. 246). IPS Empress CAD included in fatigue data (Fig. 4, p. 250).
- Feldspathic glass used as infiltrating surface layer (IPS e.max CAD Crystall Glaze Spray, E = 60 GPa) in graded LDS experiment (p. 251).
- CTE matching, chipping, HF etching protocols for feldspathic: **not stated**.

### Section 3 — Lithia-based glass–ceramics (core content of this paper)
- **Three variants** (p. 246): lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5, LDS), lithium silicate (Li2SiO3, LS), biphasic LS/LDS or LAS/LDS (LiAlSi2O6/Li2Si2O5).
- **Nucleation:** most use P2O5 as primary nucleating agent; some use ZrO2 as secondary nucleant (pp. 246–247). All commercial lithia glass–ceramics contain a Li3PO4 phase (5–15 vol%) (Table footnote a, p. 246).
- **Design trend:** reduce crystal size while preserving ≥50 vol% crystallinity "to facilitate machinability and subsequent polishing capacity" (p. 247). [NR]
- **IPS e.max CAD crystallisation pathway** (p. 247; DEMONSTRATED by in situ X-ray thermodiffractometry, Ortiz et al. 2019, Fig. 1A): blue block = Li2SiO3 crystals (~40 vol%) in glass with P2O5 nucleant and CeO2 colourant → glass partially crystallises to cristobalite (SiO2) at ~660 °C → converts at ~735 °C to Li2Si2O5 (Li2SiO3 + SiO2 → Li2Si2O5) → Ce oxide precipitates ~775 °C. Final: ~70 vol% crystalline, elongated Li2Si2O5 rods (major), equiaxed Li3PO4 blocks (minor), small cubic CeO2 crystals.
- **Obsidian (LS)** (p. 247; Ortiz et al. 2020, Fig. 1B): dendritic Li2SiO3 nanocrystals; on heating no SiO2 crystallises, no oxides precipitate, no LDS forms; nanocrystals **spheroidise by solution–reprecipitation** in softened glass → ~43 vol% submicrometre round Li2SiO3 (<500 nm) + Li3PO4 (<100 nm).
- **Suprinity PC (biphasic LS/LDS)** (p. 247; Ortiz et al. 2021, Fig. 1C): lightly sintered to form Li2SiO3 nanocrystals in "partially depolymerized zirconosilicate glass"; at ~810–820 °C glass reacts with part of Li2SiO3 to form Li2Si2O5; then Li3PO4 precipitates → ~57 vol% nanocrystalline phase in zirconosilicate glass matrix. (Note: zirconia described as part of the glass, not as discrete crystals — see Controversies.)
- **Dimensional stability on crystallisation** (p. 247): e.max CAD firing → "virtually no deformation with very small shrinkage (~0.2% vs. ~20% sintering shrinkage of zirconia)". Mechanism: keep glass viscosity high throughout crystallisation (~2 orders of magnitude above that at softening temperature); one way is to precrystallise the glass. PROPOSED/cited.
- **Strength–toughness–microstructure (Fig. 3, p. 250):** DICOR shows inverse S–T correlation as grain size increases with extended heat treatment (cited Lawn 2004). For lithia glass–ceramics, coarser microstructures (D > 2 µm; figure label D = 2.5–8 µm) give significantly higher T **and** higher S than fine-grained (D = 0.5–1 µm). Authors call this counterintuitive and **PROPOSE** that base-glass composition and glass–crystal interface properties dominate over microstructural scale (p. 250).
- Fine-grained variants for grind-and-seat without post-shaping heat treatment (Celtra Duo, Initial LiSi Block, N!CE) show relatively low S and T, "suggesting that they may actually be more susceptible to processing and handling damage" (p. 250). PROPOSED.
- Superiority of lithia over DICOR "can be partly attributed to stronger intrinsic bonding with lithium ions within the atomic superstructure and at the crystal–glass interfaces" (p. 251). PROPOSED, no data.
- **Toughening mechanisms by name (crack deflection, crack bridging): NOT STATED** in this paper. Only "toughness T … resistance to the propagation of a crack once it has formed" (p. 249) and longitudinal cracks in crowns "extend slowly and stably … can remain contained along the side walls" (p. 249, cites Zhang et al. 2016).
- **Optics:** lithia glass–ceramics have "superior aesthetics, owing to their greater translucency and range of color shade selections" vs zirconia (p. 250). [NR] Refractive-index mechanism: not stated.
- **Adhesion:** "capability to form strong adhesive resin bonding through traditional acid etching and silanization processes" (p. 250, cites Blatz et al. 2018). Etching protocol details: not stated (5% HF used only as a microscopy etch, Fig. 1 legend, p. 248).
- **Modulus matching:** lithia silicates' elastic moduli "better matched to the underlying tooth support relative to stiffer zirconia or alumina competitors, enabling greater load-bearing capacity" (p. 251, cites Ma et al. 2013 FEA). PROPOSED/cited.
- **Graded glass-infiltrated LDS** (p. 251, Fig. 5): DEMONSTRATED (lab, thesis data) ~30% higher fracture load, no adverse effect on translucency.

### Section 4 — Polycrystalline (zirconia)
- Only comparative: "Zirconia ceramics have higher strength and toughness, especially for compositions with lower yttria contents (i.e., 3 to 4 mol%…), making them the most durable crown and FDP materials (Christensen 2018)" (p. 250). [NR, cites a practice report]
- Zirconia sintering shrinkage ~20% (p. 247).
- Phases, transformation toughening, LTD, MDP: **not stated**.

### Section 5 — Fatigue
- Strength S defined as tensile stress at which catastrophic fracture initiates from flaws, intrinsic or from surface preparation (p. 249).
- **Strength values from polished specimens are "a best-case scenario"**; intaglio surface of a crown/FDP is never polished → subsurface grinding damage (p. 249).
- **CAD/CAM machining drops nominal strength by over a factor of 2** (p. 249; DEMONSTRATED in Fig. 2).
- Precrystallised blocks susceptible to grinding microcracks; restorations <0.7 mm thickness prone to catastrophic fracture during CAD/CAM grinding; margins prone to chipping (p. 248).
- **Cyclic fatigue** (p. 250, Fig. 4): "S values of these glass–ceramics (and indeed all oxide ceramics) typically degrade by up to a factor of 2 to 3 over a million loading cycles". Mechanism: "cumulative slow crack growth from intrusion of water molecules into surface flaws (Wiederhorn 1967)". DEMONSTRATED (flexure data) + mechanism cited.
- "Additional mechanisms of a mechanical nature can greatly exacerbate fatigue … especially in contact (occlusal) loading" (p. 250). [NR, cited Zhang et al. 2013]
- Static lab tests "may not provide a significant indicator of restoration longevity" (p. 250).
- Ductile-regime grinding (p. 252): region between grinding and polishing where microcracks form within the chip but do not penetrate below the cut surface; removal rate ">3 orders of magnitude faster than polishing"; "not yet been successfully applied in dentistry". PROPOSED.

### Section 6 — Summary / ceramics vs composites
- Resin composites: not discussed. Wear: not discussed (only "Micromechanics of machining and wear" in a reference title).
- Additive manufacturing of ceramics: high porosity and flaw populations diminish strength and translucency (p. 252); DIP & selective laser melting/sintering poor shape accuracy; DIP & stereolithography prolonged drying, debinding, crystallisation/sintering (p. 252).

## 5. Classification schemes

- Lithia glass–ceramics: "3 variants: lithium disilicate (Li2Si2O5—LDS), lithium silicate (Li2SiO3—LS), and biphasic LS/LDS or LAS/LDS (LiAlSi2O6/Li2Si2O5)" (p. 246).
- Table groupings (p. 246): "Predominantly Li2Si2O5" / "Predominantly Li2SiO3" / "Biphasic — Li2SiO3/Li2Si2O5; LiAlSi2O6/Li2Si2O5".
- Fabrication-route evolution (p. 247): (i) grinding partially crystallised blocks + crystallisation + glaze firing (e.max CAD, Obsidian, Suprinity PC, Amber Mill) → (ii) grinding fully crystallised blocks + post-grind glaze firing only → (iii) grinding fully crystallised with no post-grind heat treatment (Initial LiSi Block, N!CE).
- No glass/glass-ceramic/polycrystalline scheme is proposed.

## 7. Controversies / caveats raised

- Clinical survival data are inconsistent across studies; many confounders (tooth, position, restoration type, patient, dentist skill, sample size, follow-up) (p. 247).
- Long-term data almost only for IPS e.max; other variants lack systematic long-term studies (p. 248).
- Standardised tests on polished specimens "may not always be a reliable indicator of ultimate performance" (p. 249); static tests may not predict longevity (p. 250).
- Fine-grained, no-firing "grind-and-seat" materials may be more damage-susceptible (p. 250) — runs against marketing of these products.
- Counterintuitive positive correlation between crystal size and strength/toughness in lithia materials (p. 250) — explanation is speculative.
- Hand-grinding to avoid CAD/CAM chipping compromises fit; heat-press is labour-intensive (p. 248).
- AM for ceramic restorations "remains a technology in progress" (p. 252).
- **Reviewer flags (mine):** (i) Suprinity zirconia described here as part of a "zirconosilicate glass" vs other papers that describe "zirconia particles/crystals" — reconcile before teaching ZLS as "zirconia-reinforced". (ii) Celtra Duo 26% fracture figure comes from a practice-based clinicians' report, not a peer-reviewed trial. (iii) Zirconia "most durable" claim cites the same practice report. (iv) Fig. 5 data come from a master's thesis. (v) T values for most brands are from Lubauer 2022; method not stated in this paper — check the primary source before quoting K_Ic.

## 8. Use in lecture

- Use the e.max CAD reaction pathway (blue LS block ~40 vol% → cristobalite ~660 °C → LDS ~735 °C → ~70 vol% interlocked rods) as an animated "crystallisation firing" sequence (p. 247).
- Contrast three lithia microstructures (rods vs round nanocrystals vs nano-biphasic) to show that "lithium disilicate" on a label hides different structures (Fig. 1, Table, pp. 246–248).
- Teach "strength is a property of the surface, not just the material": machining halves strength, polishing/firing recover it (Fig. 2, p. 249). Clinical tie-in: intaglio surface is never polished.
- Teach strength vs toughness as distinct, and that coarser interlocked LDS crystals give higher toughness (Fig. 3, p. 250) — present the authors' explanation as a hypothesis.
- Fatigue slide: strength falls 2–3× over a million cycles in water because water assists slow crack growth at flaw tips (Fig. 4, p. 250).
- Dimensional-stability comparison: ~0.2% crystallisation shrinkage (LDS) vs ~20% sintering shrinkage (zirconia) (p. 247) — links processing to fit.
- Modulus-matching argument (LDS ~100 GPa closer to tooth than zirconia) for load-bearing on dentin (p. 251) — label as a design argument.
- Clinical anchor: 3-unit LDS FDP survival 87.9% at 10 y → 48.6% at 15 y (p. 247) — "glass–ceramics are fatigue-limited in long-span or molar use".
- Adhesion: one-line link "glass phase → HF etch + silane bonding" (p. 250); take protocol details from another source.
- Do not use this paper for feldspathic, zirconia phases, LTD, MDP, or composites — not covered.
