## 2. Evidence type

- Narrative review of **characterisation methods**: how to test, not how materials compare.
- Stated search: PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Google Scholar; English; in vitro studies, technique reports and review articles; literature up to June 2024 (Table 1, PDF p.2 [1056]). No PRISMA, no quality appraisal.
- Figures are almost all **author-drawn schematics** of test apparatus. The only experimental image is Fig. 14 (contact-angle photos).
- Gives **ISO thresholds** (ISO 6872:2023, ISO 18675:2022, ISO 9693:2019) and perception thresholds. It gives **no material-specific property values** (no K_Ic, strength, hardness or CTE for named ceramics).
- **Explicitly excludes** "the impact strength test, slow crack growth parameter, fatigue resistance test, and finite element analysis" (PDF p.31 [1085]).

## 3. Key statements by lecture section

D = stated as established/reported. P = proposed or hedged.

### Section 1 — Introduction / basic properties
- Ceramics' popularity rests on "superior biocompatibility, natural aesthetic appearance, improved mechanical performance, and established longevity" (PDF p.1 [1055]).
- Their "brittle and stochastic nature" are acknowledged shortcomings (abstract, p.1).
- "Considering the brittle nature of dental ceramics and the absence of plastic deformation processes within, intraoral occlusal stresses can lead to repercussions ranging from subcritical crack growth to catastrophic failure" (PDF p.2 [1056]).
- "Dental ceramics are weaker under tension than under compression", which makes flexural strength significant (PDF p.2). D
- Flexural tests (3-point, 4-point, ball-on-ring, ring-on-ring, piston-on-ring, piston-on-three-balls, ball-on-three-balls) all generate tensile stress on the face opposite the load. A **pre-existing critical flaw** in the max-tensile area propagates to failure (PDF p.2). D
- 3-point bending gives higher values than 4-point "due to larger flaw-containing areas in the latter" (PDF p.2). D
- **ISO 6872:2023 mechanical thresholds and clinical indications** (Table 2, PDF p.7–8 [1061–1062]). See the values table below.
- Hardness "can be used as an indicator of various properties, including wear resistance and polishability" (PDF p.10 [1064]).
- Light transmission is governed by RI match between glass and crystals. "A greater RI match results in higher light transmission, whereas large RI mismatches hinder light passage." "Higher translucency is seen in thinner restorations of high glassy content, whereas thicker and polycrystalline ceramics display greater opacity" (PDF p.17 [1071]). D
- Amorphous vs crystalline atomic structure, and bonding type: **not stated**.
- Classification glassy / glass-ceramic / polycrystalline: only implied ("polymer-infiltrated hybrid ceramics as well as lithium-based silicate glass ceramics and zirconia polycrystalline ceramics", p.1). No formal classification.

### Section 2 — Feldspathic porcelains / veneering
- "According to ISO standard 9693:2019, thermal compatibility in veneers can be predicted from individual veneering and substrate materials' coefficients of linear thermal expansion (CTE)", measured by pushrod dilatometer (PDF p.30 [1084]). D
- DSC data on glass transition and melting, "in addition to the coefficient of linear thermal expansion data — can aid in predicting dental ceramics' potential employment as veneers to underlying ceramic or metallic substrates" (PDF p.30).
- Edge chipping: "occurs as a result of the proximity of intraoral forces to the restoration edge". Chipping forces "depend on the geometry and direction of the loading component as well as the hardness and fracture toughness of the restoration" (PDF p.10 [1064]). D
- Leucite, feldspar composition, residual stresses: **not stated**.
- HF + silane on feldspathic: **not stated**. Silane appears nowhere in the paper.

### Section 3 — Lithium (di)silicate glass-ceramics
- Glass-ceramic manufacture (PDF p.29 [1083]):
  - Precursor glass is melted above liquidus and quenched below T_g "at a rate (q) faster than the critical cooling rate (q_c) to eliminate premature nucleation".
  - Then it is "heated to initiate nucleation, followed by crystal formation and growth". D
- Thermal parameters: T_g, T_n (nucleation), T_c (crystallisation), T_m (melting onset), T_L (liquidus), thermal expansion (PDF p.29). DSC/DTA show these as exo/endothermic peaks (Fig. 24).
- "Regulation of the nucleation rate has shown to determine the nature of nucleation (heterogenous versus homogenous nucleation), which in turn impacts the resultant crystallisation mode (surface versus volume crystallisation), thus considerably influencing the microstructure and, accordingly, the mechanical performance" [51] (PDF p.30 [1084]). D (cited)
- DSC "verified that the glass transition and melting temperatures of dental ceramics demonstrate an inverse relationship to their firing temperature" (PDF p.30). D, unreferenced in that sentence.
- XRD quantification: Rietveld refinement "could result" in an overestimation of crystal content for glass-containing ceramics. The **G-factor method** with an external quartz standard gives absolute crystalline and amorphous fractions [27, 135] (PDF p.26–27 [1080–1081]). D
- Fig. 15 depicts light pathways through a "lithium silicate-based ceramic" with elongated crystals (PDF p.17).
- **HF etching:** only Fig. 14. On the intaglio of a zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Celtra Duo), saline contact angle is 39.42° untreated, 18.36° after 5 % HF and 76.28° after self-etch primer (photographs, PDF p.17). "The extent of wettability and surface energy of the intaglio ceramic surfaces can predict their bond strength and adhesion performance" [80] (PDF p.16–17). P/D
  - Note: ZLS, not pure LD.
- Crystal fraction, crystal size or morphology for LD; crack deflection or bridging specific to LD: **not stated**.
- R-curve: toughening mechanisms "such as crack shielding, crack bridging, crack branching, and phase transformation, are able to enhance the fracture toughness ... as the crack dimension increases". "Ceramics exhibiting R-curve behaviour are deemed more reliable (less scatter in strength values) and of predictable longevity" (PDF p.8 [1062]). D (general, cited [1,31,32])
- Wear of LD vs enamel: **not stated** (refs on it are listed, e.g. Lawson 2014, PDF p.33, but there is no content).

### Section 4 — Polycrystalline (alumina, zirconia)
- "Polycrystalline zirconia and alumina machinable blocks can be supplied as powder-pressed (i.e., green blanks) or partially sintered, which inevitably results in **20–35% volumetric shrinkage** upon complete sintering" (PDF p.8 [1062]). D
- Shrinkage factor d = (d_w + d_b + d_l)/3, with d_x = pre/post dimension (eq. 10). Warpage e = (c/x) × 100 (eq. 11, Fig. 6) (PDF p.8–9). D
- Translucency of polycrystalline ceramics (PDF p.19 [1073]):
  - It "is significantly impacted by the grain size and density, as light scattering occurs along the grain boundaries".
  - "In order to foster translucency within polycrystalline ceramics, grain sizes are increased to reduce the grain boundaries and, in turn, lessen light scatter" [96,97]. Stated as fact.
  - **Simplification:** no mention of cubic phase or birefringence.
- "Crystals and flaws smaller than the visible-light wavelength (350–800 nm) allow complete light transmission and result in a transparent glass, while larger crystals and flaws will scatter and reflect light waves, creating a translucent ceramic" (PDF p.19).
- Opacity "is controlled by the crystalline structure, grain size, flaw distribution, thickness, and firing parameters ... as well as the colour of the underlying substrate and shade of the resin luting cement" [108] (PDF p.21 [1075]).
- Point defects such as oxygen vacancies "can enhance light absorption and should be eliminated through adequate sintering protocols" [103] (PDF p.20 [1074]).
- Surface treatments listed: "silicatisation, 10 MPD coating [sic], laser irradiation, and plasma application" (PDF p.24–25 [1078–1079]). No mechanism is given.
- Raman can identify "phases, and phase transformations" at 0.01–2300 μm depth (PDF p.28 [1082]). Monoclinic/tetragonal detection is not specified.
- **Not stated:**
  - m/t/c phases and temperatures
  - yttria content
  - 3Y/4Y/5Y
  - transformation toughening mechanism (only listed by name as an R-curve mechanism, p.8)
  - t→m volume change
  - LTD
  - MDP chemistry

### Section 5 — Fatigue / reliability
- **Weibull** (PDF p.4–5 [1058–1059]):
  - Strength depends on "the size and distribution of an intrinsic flaw population". "Larger sized flaws are more likely to cause failure yet are not normally distributed". Strength is therefore analysed by Weibull rather than Gaussian statistics.
  - P_f(σ) = 1 − exp[−(σ/σ₀)^m] (eq. 4). σ₀ is the characteristic strength at 63.2 % failure probability; m is the Weibull modulus.
  - "A higher m is associated with a steeper curve and a narrow distribution of flaws, indicating higher reliability".
  - Confidence intervals per EN 843-5:2007. Unbiasing m̂_U = m̂ × UF per ISO 20501:2022 (eq. 5). Large samples are needed. D
- **Size effect** (PDF p.5): "larger sized specimens will have a greater likelihood of enclosing larger inherent flaws, thereby resulting in lower flexural strength". Direct comparison across test geometries "violates the principles of Weibull probability and thus should be avoided". Scaling uses effective volume or surface (eqs. 6–7). D
- **K_Ic definition** (PDF p.5–6 [1059–1060]):
  - Griffith: stress concentrates at the flaw tip, proportional to flaw length. Irwin: fracture when K > K_Ic.
  - Fracture modes I/II/III (Fig. 3).
  - Test methods: SEVNB, SCF, DCB, CT, SEPB, CNSR, NTP, and indentation (Fig. 4).
  - Indentation K_Ic values show "48% variations from their true K_Ic values" [15,26]. Indentation methods "can provide a comparison between materials but cannot identify the exact fracture toughness value" (PDF p.6). D
- B3B-K_Ic with a Knoop pre-crack gives results "comparable ... to standard K_Ic measuring techniques" [27–29] (PDF p.6–7). D
- **Subcritical crack growth** "is a time-dependent phenomenon wherein long-term repetitive loading fosters strength degradation" (PDF p.7–8 [1061–1062]). D (one sentence; no SCG equation and no n-value)
- "Fracture toughness data of dental ceramics has been reported to be a better predictor of their clinical longevity than flexural strength data, as the former can yield information related to unstable crack growth" [51] (PDF p.12 [1066]). P/D (single secondary ref)
- Flexural tests are quasi-static and give no information on "crack resistance and deformation during high-strain-rate loading" (PDF p.12).
- "The implementation of artificial aging prior to the mechanical characterisation of dental ceramics can complement the clinical applicability" (PDF p.12).
- **S–N curves, Paris law, cyclic fatigue protocols: not stated.** Fatigue testing is explicitly excluded (p.31).

### Section 6 — Ceramics vs resin composites; wear; optics summary
- B3B caution: "Materials with lower elastic moduli, such as dental composites, exhibit greater deformations and a larger contact area than materials with higher elastic moduli, such as ceramics" (PDF p.3 [1057]). This is the **only direct ceramic–composite comparison** in the paper (stiffness).
- Wear (PDF p.12–13 [1066–1067]):
  - Influences: humidity, pH, load cycles, temperature, antagonist, toothbrush, mouthwash, bruxism, GERD, acidic drinks.
  - Tribometers: pin-on-disc, ball-on-3-flat, sphere-on-plate, ball-crater, chewing simulators (Fig. 10).
  - "Reduced wear potential of dental ceramics is found when their COF resembles that of the natural dentition" [55].
  - "The wear rate of dental ceramics depends on their microstructures, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, as well as the occlusal forces and opposing substrate material" [59]. D
- No chewing simulator reproduces full arches. "Exact values should not be compared; instead, trends should be observed" (PDF p.14 [1068]).
- Chemical solubility is per ISO 6872:2023 (values below) (PDF p.14).
- Surface roughness ">0.2 μm" is associated with "higher plaque accumulation, antagonistic wear of the natural dentition, and human tactile perceptibility" [63–65] (PDF p.14). D
- Optics:
  - Smoother surfaces reflect, rougher scatter, giving a darker appearance (PDF p.17).
  - Fluorescence comes from rare-earth oxides (terbium, cerium, ytterbium). Repeated firing reduces it. Dense crystalline frameworks shift the tint to yellow-green (PDF p.21).
  - Opalescence is selective scattering in the glass phase (PDF p.21).
  - Increased opacity is "considered the main cause of their aesthetic failure" [107] (PDF p.21).
- **Ceramics vs composites for wear or crystallinity–optics: not stated** beyond the above.

## 6. Controversies, caveats and unsupported assertions

1. **Fig. 16 b* axis labels are inverted** relative to the colours and to the paper's own definition (b* > 0 = yellowness, p.18). Do not reuse as is.
2. **Hardness ranges** (p.10): micro "depth ≥ 0.2 mm" and nano "< 0.2 mm". The units as printed look implausible for micro/nano-indentation. Verify against the primary standard before teaching. Not cited in that sentence.
3. **Opalescence** "arises when the mismatch between the refractive indices of crystalline and glassy phases is 1.1" (p.21, one ref [110]). The physical meaning of a fixed "1.1" is unclear as printed. Do not teach this number.
4. **Eq. 13** is printed as G_IC = K_Ic/E (p.9, confirmed on the rendered page). This may be a typesetting error (an exponent may be missing). Check it against a fracture-mechanics textbook before teaching. Not needed for this lecture.
5. **PT/AT colour thresholds** (p.18). The printed ordering ("PT and AT are 1.0, 1.7, and 3.1, and 6.0, 3.7, and 1.8") gives AT decreasing as agreement % rises, which is ambiguous. Quote only from the primary source [87] if needed (better in L04 Colour).
6. **XRF "cannot identify" Na, Mg, Al, Si, P** (p.27). Stated without nuance, and presented as general. Irrelevant to this lecture.
7. **Translucency of polycrystals** (p.19): "grain sizes are increased to reduce the grain boundaries". This is a simplification. Cubic-phase content and birefringence, the key 4Y/5Y concepts, are **not mentioned**. Do not use this paper to teach 3Y vs 5Y.
8. **"Fracture toughness ... better predictor of clinical longevity than flexural strength"** (p.12) rests on a single citation [51], a glass-science review, not clinical data.
9. **"DSC ... verified that the glass transition and melting temperatures ... demonstrate an inverse relationship to their firing temperature"** (p.30). No citation in the sentence and no data shown.
10. **Fig. 14** is ZLS (Celtra Duo), not lithium disilicate. Image source not stated. Single example, n not stated.
11. Fig. 25 dilatometer display value (32.6 × 10⁻⁶/°C) is decorative. Do not cite.
12. The review explicitly **does not cover** slow crack growth parameters, fatigue tests or FEA (p.31). It cannot serve as a fatigue (S–N) source.
13. Typos: "10 MPD coating" (p.24–25, presumably 10-MDP); "CAM/CAM" (p.3); "CAD/CAD" (p.12); "LUC" for LCU (Fig. 19 caption).

## 7. Use in lecture

- **§1 / §6 summary — "Which ceramic for which indication?"** Rebuild Table 2 (ISO 6872:2023 thresholds, p.7–8) as an interactive ladder: 50/0.7 → 100/1.0 → 300/2.0 → 500/3.5 → 800/5.0, mapped to anterior → posterior → 3-unit → molar 3-unit → ≥4 units. Students drag material classes onto rungs. (Material values must come from other sources, because this paper gives none.)
- **§1 brittle behaviour:** "absence of plastic deformation" and "weaker under tension than under compression" (p.2), with Fig. 1 (tension on the face opposite the load) and Fig. 3a (mode I).
- **§5 Weibull:** P_f = 1 − exp[−(σ/σ₀)^m]; σ₀ at 63.2 %; higher m means a narrower flaw distribution and higher reliability; bigger specimens fail at lower stress (p.5). Pair with Denry Fig. 2 (flaw population). Build an m-slider showing two materials with the same mean strength but different m.
- **§5 R-curve:** shielding, bridging, branching and phase transformation raise toughness as the crack grows. R-curve ceramics are "more reliable (less scatter)" (p.8). This is a bridge between LD (§3) and zirconia (§4).
- **§5 method literacy:** indentation K_Ic deviates by up to 48 % from true values (p.6). Use this as a "critically appraise manufacturers' claims" moment (Course LO 6).
- **§3 glass-ceramic processing:** quench above q_c → nucleate → crystallise (p.29), with Fig. 24 (DSC: T_g, T_c, T_m). Pair with Denry Fig. 9 (metasilicate → disilicate) to anchor the curve to a real microstructure.
- **§3 etching and adhesion:** Fig. 14 shows 5 % HF dropping the contact angle from 39° to 18° on a lithium-silicate glass-ceramic (p.17). Wettability predicts bonding (p.16–17). Note that silane is **not stated** here and needs another source.
- **§1 / §3 optics:** Fig. 15 (six light pathways in a lithium-silicate microstructure) plus the RI-match rule and the "thin + glassy = translucent; thick + polycrystalline = opaque" statement (p.17).
- **§6 wear:** wear rate depends on microstructure, modulus, K_Ic, load and antagonist (p.13). Roughness >0.2 μm increases plaque and antagonist wear (p.14). Use Fig. 10 to show how wear is tested. Add a caveat: compare trends, not exact values (p.14).
- **Chemical solubility thresholds (ISO 6872:2023, p.14):** ≤100 μg/cm² for veneering/monolithic and ≤2000 μg/cm² for frameworks. A short "degradation" fact for §2/§6.
