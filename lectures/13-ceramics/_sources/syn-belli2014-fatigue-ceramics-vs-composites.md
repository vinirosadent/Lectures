## 2. Evidence type

**Original in vitro experimental study** (laboratory comparison of 5 ceramics and 5 resin composites; 4-point bending static strength + staircase cyclic fatigue). Includes a brief narrative discussion citing clinical annual fracture rates from other studies. Note: data for three composites (Clearfil Majesty Posterior, Grandio SO, Tetric EvoCeram) are "Results taken from Ref. [15]" (Tables 3–4, p. 429 [PDF 6]).

## 3. Key mechanistic statements

Labels: **DEMONSTRATED (this study)** = measured here; **CITED** = from another referenced study; **PROPOSED** = authors' interpretation ("expected", "probably", "hypothesize", "seems"). **RETRACTED** = withdrawn in corrigendum.

### Study design (for interpreting numbers)
- Bars 2 × 2 × 25 mm; stored 14 days in distilled water at 37 °C; 4-point bending; σ_in n = 15 per material at 0.75 mm/min; σ_ff by staircase, 10⁴ cycles, 0.5 Hz, n = 25, stress alternating between 1 MPa and σ_app; first specimen at σ_app = 0.5 σ_in; all in distilled water at 37 °C (pp. 424, 426–427 [PDF 1, 3–4]).
- Ceramic tensile surfaces polished to 4000 grit SiC (p. 426 [PDF 3]).
- Processing: e.max CAD crystallised at max 850 °C; ZirCAD sintered 2 h at 1530 °C (200 °C/h); e.max Press via lost-wax, sandblasted with 50 µm glass particles at 2 bar; e.max Ceram sintered at max 750 °C, water-jet cut (6000 bar, 1000 m/s) (pp. 426–427 [PDF 3–4]).

### Results (DEMONSTRATED, this study)
- All materials showed significant strength degradation after cyclic loading (p. 428 [PDF 5]).
- ZirCAD: highest σ_in and σ_ff (768 and 440 MPa) (p. 424 abstract; Table 4 p. 429).
- Lithium disilicate: CAD and Press "similar in the static test", but Press "showed a significantly higher fatigue resistance after cyclic loading" (σ_ff 202.7 vs 120.8 MPa) (pp. 424, 428 [PDF 1, 5]).
- Fluorapatite glass-ceramic (e.max Ceram) and feldspathic porcelain (Trilux Forte): equivalent initial and cyclic properties; σ0 under 70 MPa, "statistically lower than all other ceramic and resin composite materials" (pp. 424, 428 [PDF 1, 5]).
- Composites σ0 92–145 MPa; most highly filled direct composite (Clearfil Majesty Posterior) best fatigue performance among composites (σ_ff 81.4 MPa) (pp. 424, 428 [PDF 1, 5]).
- Lowest strength loss: e.max Press 29.6% and Clearfil Majesty Posterior 32.0%; highest: CeramX Duo 62.3% (pp. 424, 429 [PDF 1, 6]).
- Conclusion: "Dental ceramics and resin composite materials show equivalent fatigue strength degradation at loads around 0.5 σ_in values." (p. 424 [PDF 1]); "Under the same stress levels relative to the initial flexural strength, resin composites and dental ceramics present similar fatigue degradation." (p. 431 [PDF 8]).
- "Resin composite materials used for direct restorations are more fatigue resistant than glass-rich ceramics used for indirect restorations in cyclic flexural loading. Direct and indirect resin composites have shown comparable fatigue performances. Zirconium dioxide and lithium disilicate-based ceramics showed the highest initial and residual fatigue strength" (p. 431 [PDF 8]).

### Mechanisms (mostly PROPOSED or CITED)
- Strength degradation severity "showed a high dependency on composition and microstructure, indicating the operation of distinct degradation mechanisms" (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — interpretation of DEMONSTRATED data.
- "Common to all glass-containing ceramics, stress corrosion acts as the dominant mechanism reducing their fracture resistance and clinical lifetime." (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — PROPOSED/literature-based.
- Degradation in e.max CAD, Press, Ceram and Trilux Forte "are expected to have been governed by typical stress corrosion mechanisms, with strength degradation severity being linked to their glass phase amount." (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — PROPOSED. (Glass-amount reasoning for CAD vs Press later withdrawn — see corrigendum.)
- Low glass-content alumina–glass and alumina–zirconia–glass composites: n_dyn "generally orders of magnitude higher than in glass-rich feldspathic porcelains and silicate-based glass-ceramics" (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — CITED [7,26,27].
- In e.max Press, cracks propagate preferentially through the glass phase, but "lithium disilicate crystals are efficient in promoting crack deflection and crack branching" (extrinsic toughening), unlike leucite- and fluorapatite-containing glass-ceramics (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — CITED [9–11,28] + PROPOSED as explanation for 29.6% loss.
- "Differences between e.max Press and e.max CAD are probably related to the higher amount of glass, reduced crystal phase and small crystal size of the latter." (p. 429 [PDF 6]) — PROPOSED; **glass-amount part RETRACTED** by corrigendum (p. 1204 [PDF 11]): "is speculated to be in part related to their difference in glass phase content. This is obviously wrong". Crystal-size difference (Table 2: CAD 0.2–2 µm vs Press 3–6 µm) is not addressed by the corrigendum.
- Conclusion sentence "For the lithium disilicate ceramics, the amount of glass phase (therefore the amount of crystals) is determinant in their fatigue behavior" (p. 431 [PDF 8]) — **RETRACTED** by corrigendum.
- Low m and high SD of σ_ff for e.max Press "might reflect an inhomogeneous distribution of large processing flaws" (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — PROPOSED.
- Zirconia: cracks "accelerated by corrosion of Zr–O bonds in the presence of moisture [31], which also seems to act by prompting spontaneous transformation of crystals that loose the potential for toughening [32]" (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — CITED/PROPOSED ("seems"). Relevant to §4 LTD concept.
- "In cyclic fatigue, friction of the fractured surfaces consists of an additional degradation mechanism leading to higher crack growth susceptibility than under static fatigue loading conditions (n_cyc < n_sta) [33]." (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — CITED.
- "For stabilized zirconium dioxide, the susceptibility to cyclic fatigue effects seems to be directly associated to the potential for transformation toughening [34]." (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — CITED/PROPOSED.
- Composites: lower degradation with higher filler content, "reflecting the susceptibility of the resin phase to strain challenges [14] and environmental degradation [35]" (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — PROPOSED ("allows us to hypothesize").
- Larger particles → superior K development during crack growth than nanofilled composites [40]; may explain Clearfil Majesty Posterior > Lava Ultimate (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — PROPOSED.
- Tetric EvoCeram > Grandio SO in fatigue "due to its large pre-polymerized filler phase" [15] (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — CITED.
- Resin composites: "higher initial fracture toughness and superior R-curve behavior" than glass-rich ceramics (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — CITED/stated.
- Fast-fracture properties (modulus, strength, toughness) "are particularly relevant during overloading events that lead to fast fracture. This scenario is however not that representative of clinical failures" (p. 425 [PDF 2]) — CITED [2,12,13].
- In resin composites, fast-fracture properties "have shown little association to their fatigue resistance under cyclic loading in vitro [15]. For ceramic restorations, in vitro fatigue experiments have shown to be good predictors of their in vivo survivability [16]." (p. 425 [PDF 2]) — CITED.
- n_dyn (dynamic method) is "suboptimal" for comparing composites with ceramics because of composites' "substantial viscoelastic response … at low strain rates" (p. 425 [PDF 2]).
- Clinical failure of small–medium posterior restorations mostly by secondary caries (biological) or fracture (mechanical) [2] (p. 425 [PDF 2]) — CITED.
- Resin composites "contract", may stress cavity walls, "more technique sensitive, color unstable and less wear resistant than their ceramic restoration counterparts" (p. 425 [PDF 2]) — stated without data (Introduction). **Only statement in the three papers relevant to wear of ceramics vs composites; no wear data.**
- Inlay fracture influenced by luting material and operator experience; operator effect linked to "increased surface roughness after contact adjustment, which eventually leads to early fractures of the low-toughness glass-ceramic" (p. 431 [PDF 8]) — CITED [44,52].

## 7. Controversies / caveats

- **Corrigendum (p. 1204 [PDF 11])**: e.max CAD glass content is ~30% after final crystallization (not 60%); the authors state the glass-content explanation for CAD vs Press fatigue difference "is obviously wrong". The paper's conclusion on glass amount in LD fatigue is therefore invalid. Any slide must use the corrected value.
- **Equal % degradation ≠ equal clinical behaviour**: stronger materials were tested at much higher absolute stresses (485 MPa ZirCAD vs normal chewing loads); under normal chewing loads "a much lower fatigue degradation and longer clinical lifespan would be expected" for zirconia and LD (p. 430 [PDF 7]) — PROPOSED.
- **Only 10⁴ cycles at 0.5 Hz** — short fatigue challenge; σ_ff defined for that number only (pp. 424, 428).
- Bars, polished, in water — not restorations; "Within the limits of our methodology and the selection of materials tested here" (p. 431 [PDF 8]).
- Three composite datasets reused from Ref. [15] (p. 429).
- Clinical recommendation "Resin composite restorations may be used as an equivalent alternative to glass-rich-ceramic inlays regarding mechanical performance" (p. 425 [PDF 2]) rests on lab data + cited annual fracture rates; authors note operator and luting influence clinical fracture (p. 431).
- Fast-fracture properties poorly predict composite fatigue in vitro [15] (p. 425).
- Wear claim ("less wear resistant than their ceramic … counterparts", p. 425) is an unsupported introductory statement — not tested.

## 8. Use in lecture

1. **§1 classification by glass content**: Table 2 (with corrigendum) — zirconia 0%, lithium disilicate ~30%, feldspathic 80%, fluorapatite up to 90% glass (p. 427; p. 1204).
2. **§1/§6 property ranges**: modulus 65–250 GPa (ceramics) vs 8–15 GPa (composites); K_Ic 0.7–1.5 (glass-rich) / ~3 (LD) / 5–7 (zirconia, alumina) / 1–2 (composites) MPa√m (p. 425) — cited values; mark as approximate literature ranges.
3. **§2 feldspathic**: Trilux Forte σ_in ~66 MPa, very high m (22.5) — weak but very consistent; strength similar to/below composites (pp. 428–429).
4. **§3 lithium disilicate**: same chemistry, different processing (Press vs CAD) → similar initial strength but different fatigue strength (202.8 vs 120.9 MPa) (p. 428). Use carefully: authors' glass-content explanation retracted; crystal size differs (3–6 vs 0.2–2 µm, Table 2).
5. **§4 zirconia**: highest σ_in 768 MPa and σ_ff 440 MPa but 42.7% loss (p. 429); moisture corrodes Zr–O bonds and may trigger spontaneous transformation that removes toughening potential (p. 430) → bridge to LTD.
6. **§5 Weibull**: σ0 at 63.2%, σ at 5%, m describes scatter (p. 427); Press m 3.45 vs Trilux 22.5 (p. 429) → "strong ≠ reliable".
7. **§5 fatigue headline**: every material, ceramic or composite, lost ~30–62% of its strength after only 10,000 cycles in water (Table 4, p. 429).
8. **§6 ceramics vs composites**: glass-rich ceramics are weaker and degrade more than direct composites in this test; LD and zirconia remain far stronger (p. 431). Clinical annual fracture rates of inlays vs direct composites are all ~0.06–1.1% (p. 431) — cited, heterogeneous.
9. **§6 misconception box**: "Ceramic is always stronger than composite" — false for glass-rich ceramics (σ0 < 70 MPa vs composites 92–145 MPa; p. 428).
10. **Critical-thinking slide**: corrigendum as a teaching example of how published interpretations get corrected (p. 1204).
