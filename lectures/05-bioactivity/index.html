Bioactivity

What it really means for a dental material to be “bioactive” — why the concept is broader than forming apatite, and why the word is worth more than the label on the box.

The concept

A bioactive material does not just sit safely in the tooth — it provokes a specific, intended, beneficial biological response.

That one idea separates it from three neighbours it is often confused with. Flip each card.

Two definitions, one idea

Narrow (historical): Hench’s original meaning — a material that forms an apatite layer on its surface and bonds to living tissue. Broad (modern): a material that elicits a specific, predictable, intended biological response — stimulating or directing cells and tissue, or controlling microbes. The narrow case is just one member of the broad family.

Deep dive · how the standards word it

ISO standards tie bioactivity mostly to implants: a property that elicits a specific biological response at the material–tissue interface, forming a bond (ISO 23317 tests apatite-forming ability in simulated body fluid). Biomaterials bodies phrase it more broadly — materials “designed to stimulate and/or direct appropriate cellular and tissue responses, or control interactions with microbiological species.” Dentistry sits between the two, which is exactly why the term needs care.

Broadening the idea

Apatite formation is one classic sign of bioactivity — not the whole of it.

Historically the word was born from bioactive glass forming apatite and bonding to bone. In the mouth, the useful responses are wider: remineralisation, pulp repair, and antibacterial action all count. Bioactivity is a spectrum of biological effects, not a single test.

Myth vs fact
Myth
“It releases ions, so it’s bioactive.”
Fact
Ion release is almost a prerequisite — but it is not enough. Some materials release calcium yet form no apatite and drive no tissue response. The effect, not the release, is what counts.

Mechanism — in principle

A bioactive material changes its local chemistry — releasing ions and shifting pH — until the surroundings respond.

Deep dive · the two canonical chemistries (optional)

Bioactive glass is a low-silica glass built to react: in water it exchanges ions and raises pH, a silica-rich gel forms at the surface, and calcium and phosphate then crystallise on it as a hydroxycarbonate apatite layer.

Calcium-silicate cements (MTA, Biodentine) set by reacting with water; that reaction releases calcium hydroxide, giving a high pH and a supply of calcium — which drives apatite formation and, in the tooth, a dentine-repair response. The detailed reactions are beyond this lecture; the three-beat pattern above is the point.

Routes of bioactivity

Bioactivity reaches the tissue by three routes: biological, mixed, or chemical.

This is how the FDI organises the field — a simple map from “pure chemistry” to “pure biology”.

Why this matters clinically

The route decides what evidence you should demand and how it is regulated. A purely chemical fluoride effect is judged very differently from a growth factor that actively reprograms cells — and the same material can act through more than one route at once.

A short history

The word travelled from bonding bone in the 1970s to healing pulp today — and picked up hype along the way.

Step through how the meaning of “bioactive” shifted over time.

Origin · bone

Hench invents Bioglass (45S5). “Bioactive” is coined for a glass that forms an apatite layer and bonds chemically to bone. The meaning is narrow: surface apatite → bonding to hard tissue, born in orthopaedics.

Adoption · dentistry

The idea enters the mouth. Calcium-silicate cements (MTA) reach endodontics; bioactive-glass toothpastes arrive (from ~2004) for sensitivity and remineralisation. The meaning broadens beyond apatite to ion release, remineralisation and pulp repair.

Expansion · the label spreads

“Bioactive” appears everywhere — on composites, liners and cements — often on the strength of a single ion-release study. The term becomes a marketing asset faster than the evidence behind it.

Clarification · consensus

The field pushes back. Editorials, the 2018 Northern Lights consensus and the 2022 FDI Policy Statement demand a defined mechanism and proof of effect. The mature meaning: a specific, intended, proven biological response — not a slogan.

An important distinction

Bioactive materials mostly repair tissue — they rarely regenerate the original structure.

Read the outcome carefully

Remineralised enamel is often fluorapatite — chemically a little different from what was lost. A dentine bridge under a pulp cap seals the pulp but is not native, tubular dentine. Both are genuine, valuable repair. Calling them “regeneration” overstates what the material did.

The clinical anchor · vital pulp therapy

Back to the deep cavity. A calcium-silicate cement over the near-exposed pulp releases calcium and holds a high pH; this environment recruits and differentiates odontoblast-like cells, which lay down a dentine bridge that seals the pulp. That measurable, intended, tissue-level response — not the label — is what makes MTA and Biodentine genuinely bioactive.

Reading the evidence

“Bioactive” is earned by evidence — ion release or an apatite layer in a beaker is only a screening signal.

Myth vs fact
Myth
“It’s labelled bioactive, so it must work.”
Fact
The FDI asks for a defined mechanism, a proven effect (ideally clinical), a stated duration, and no harm to the material’s main job. Materials marketed as “bioactive” have failed clinically — one cracked teeth within a year; another failed in about 24% of restorations at one year.
Why this matters clinically

Bioactivity has a price. The effects come from substances leaving the material into water, so a very reactive material also takes up water and can soften or dissolve. The same water that releases the useful ions can degrade the restoration — so “more bioactive” is not automatically better.

Deep dive · the five FDI criteria for calling a material “bioactive”
  • The mechanism is clearly defined (biological, mixed, or chemical).
  • The bioactive effect is scientifically proven — in vitro or in situ, and preferably in clinical studies.
  • The duration of the effect is stated (especially for antibacterial action).
  • There are no significant adverse effects (including driving antimicrobial resistance).
  • The prime purpose — rebuilding lost tooth structure — is not impaired, shown by clinical data.

Take-home

Bioactivity — the essentials

  1. Bioactive = an active, wanted biological response — not merely “does no harm” (that’s biocompatibility). [obj 1]
  2. It is a spectrum: apatite formation, remineralisation, pulp repair and antibacterial action all count — apatite is one example, not the definition. [obj 2]
  3. The pattern is always the same three beats: dissolve → change the local chemistry (ions, pH) → a mineral or tissue response follows. [obj 3]
  4. Three routes — chemical, mixed, biological — and the concept broadened from bone-bonding to pulp healing, which is why the term gets overused. [obj 2–4]
  5. Mostly repair, not regeneration: tertiary dentine and fluor-apatite restore function without rebuilding the original tissue. [obj 5]
  6. The word is earned by evidence: ion release or SBF apatite is screening; established calcium-silicate cements (MTA, Biodentine) carry the strongest clinical case. [obj 6]
