2

The concept

A material that makes the tissue respond

Bioactive
It does not just sit safely in the tooth — it provokes a specific, intended, beneficial biological response.
  • The response might be mineral formation, pulp repair, or controlling microbes.
  • Contrast an inert material: it is tolerated, but it does nothing.
Keep in mind “Bioactive” describes an effect on tissue — not just a property of the material sitting in the bottle.

Don’t confuse the terms

Bioactive vs its three neighbours

Bioactive

  • Actively drives a specific, wanted tissue response.

Biocompatible

  • Does no harm — safe, tolerated. Can be completely inert.

Remineralising

  • One outcome of bioactivity — rebuilding lost mineral.

Antimicrobial

  • Another possible bioactive effect — controlling bacteria.

Two definitions, one idea

Narrow vs broad — and why it matters

Narrow (historical)
Hench’s original meaning — a material that forms an apatite layer on its surface and bonds to living tissue.
Broad (modern)
A material that elicits a specific, predictable, intended biological response — stimulating or directing cells and tissue, or controlling microbes.
The point The narrow case is just one member of the broad family.

More than apatite

A spectrum of effects· ion release is a signal, not proof

Broadening the idea

Apatite formation is one sign — not the whole

  • The word was born from bioactive glass forming apatite and bonding to bone.
  • In the mouth the useful responses are wider: remineralisation, pulp repair, antibacterial action.
  • Bioactivity is a spectrum of effects, not a single test.
  • Apatite sits near the bioactive end — a marker, not the definition.

FIG 06.1 Bioactivity as a spectrum of reactivity, from inert to strongly bioactive.

Myth vs fact

Ion release is a signal — not proof

Necessary, not sufficient Ion release is almost a prerequisite for bioactivity — but on its own it proves nothing.
  • Some materials release calcium yet form no apatite and drive no tissue response.
  • The effect, not the release, is what earns the word.

How bioactivity arises

The three-beat pattern· the two canonical chemistries

Mechanism — in principle

The same three-beat pattern, whatever the material

  • Dissolve — water reaches the surface; the material releases ions (calcium, phosphate, hydroxide/fluoride).
  • Change the local chemistry — pH rises and the fluid becomes supersaturated.
  • A response follows — an apatite layer precipitates, or cells lay down new mineralised tissue.

FIG 06.3 Dissolve → change local chemistry (ions, pH) → a response follows.

The point The response — the third beat — is what “bioactive” actually names.

Deep dive · optional

The two canonical bioactive chemistries

Bioactive glass
A low-silica glass built to react: it exchanges ions and raises pH, a silica-rich gel forms, and calcium + phosphate crystallise on it as a hydroxycarbonate apatite layer.
Calcium-silicate cements
MTA and Biodentine set by reacting with water, releasing calcium hydroxide (high pH + calcium) — which drives apatite and, in the tooth, a dentine-repair response.
Same pattern Different chemistry, but both run the three beats above. The detailed reactions are beyond this lecture.

How it works — and how the idea grew

Three routes: chemical / mixed / biological· a short history

Routes of bioactivity

Three routes: chemical, mixed, or biological

  • Purely chemical — ion release / precipitation (glass-ionomer fluoride).
  • Mixed — a chemical trigger unlocks biology: Ca(OH)₂, MTA, Biodentine → tertiary dentine.
  • Solely biological — signalling molecules act on cells (growth factors; mostly experimental).
Why it matters The route decides what evidence to demand and how it is regulated.

FIG 06.4 From pure chemistry to pure biology, with established examples.

A short history

From bonding bone to healing pulp

1969–71 · Origin (bone)

Hench’s Bioglass (45S5): “bioactive” = forms apatite and bonds to bone. Narrow, born in orthopaedics.

1990s–2000s · Into the mouth

MTA reaches endodontics; bioactive-glass toothpastes arrive (~2004). The meaning broadens to ion release, remineralisation and pulp repair.

2010s · The label spreads

“Bioactive” appears everywhere — often on a single ion-release study. Marketing outruns the evidence.

2018–2022 · Consensus

The Northern Lights conference and the FDI Policy Statement demand a defined mechanism and proof. Mature meaning: specific, intended, proven.

In the tooth: repair & clinical materials

Repair vs regeneration· the established materials

An important distinction

Repair, not regeneration

  • Bioactive materials mostly repair — they rarely rebuild the original structure.
  • Remineralised enamel is often fluorapatite; a dentine bridge is osteodentin-like, not tubular.
Read the claim Both are genuine, valuable repair. Calling them “regeneration” overstates what the material did.

FIG 06.5 Repair uses substitute tissue; regeneration rebuilds the original architecture.

The clinical anchor · vital pulp therapy

How a calcium-silicate cement heals the pulp

  • Over a near-exposed pulp, the cement releases calcium and holds a high pH.
  • This recruits and differentiates odontoblast-like cells.
  • They lay down a dentine bridge that seals the pulp.
The takeaway That measurable tissue response — not the label — is what makes MTA and Biodentine genuinely bioactive.

FIG 06.6 Ca²⁺ + high pH → odontoblast-like cells → a new dentine bridge.

Established materials

Where bioactivity shows up in the clinic

  • Calcium hydroxide — OH⁻ / high pH, antibacterial, frees growth factors · pulp capping (classic).
  • MTA — Ca²⁺, OH⁻, apatite, dentine bridge · pulp capping, apexification, root-end repair.
  • Biodentine — tricalcium silicate; dentine bridge; resin-free · dentine replacement, pulpotomy.
  • Bioceramic sealers — calcium silicate; apatite at the interface · root-canal obturation.
  • Glass-ionomer cement — fluoride (rechargeable) · caries-prone & root restorations, liners.
  • Bioactive glass (NovaMin, BioMin) — Ca + phosphate; HCA layer; occludes tubules · remineralising / desensitising toothpaste.

06

Judging a “bioactive” claim

The evidence bar· the cost· take-home

Reading the evidence

“Bioactive” is earned — the FDI bar

  • A clearly defined mechanism (biological, mixed, or chemical).
  • A proven effect — in vitro / in situ, and preferably clinical.
  • A stated duration of the effect (especially antibacterial).
  • No significant adverse effects (including antimicrobial resistance).
  • The primary function — rebuilding tooth structure — is not impaired.
Reality check Materials marketed as “bioactive” have failed clinically — one cracked teeth within a year; another failed in about 24% of restorations at one year.

The trade-off

Bioactivity has a price

Structure → property → performance The effects come from substances leaving the material into water. The same water that releases useful ions can make the material take up water and soften or dissolve.
So “more bioactive” is not automatically better — reactivity that helps the tissue can cost mechanical integrity.

Take-home

Bioactivity — the essentials

1
Bioactive = an active, wanted biological response — not merely “does no harm” (that’s biocompatibility).
2
It is a spectrum: apatite, remineralisation, pulp repair, antibacterial — apatite is one example, not the definition.
3
Three beats: dissolve → change the local chemistry (ions, pH) → a mineral or tissue response follows.
4
Three routes — chemical, mixed, biological — and the concept broadened from bone to pulp, which invites overuse.
5
Mostly repair, not regeneration — tertiary dentine and fluor-apatite restore function, not the original tissue.
6
Earned by evidence — ion release / SBF apatite is screening; MTA & Biodentine carry the strongest clinical case.
