Composition

From mineral gypsum to dental hemihydrate

Gypsum products
Dental powders composed essentially of calcium sulphate hemihydrate (CaSO₄·½H₂O) that, mixed with water, set into calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO₄·2H₂O) — the same mineral, gypsum, that the powder was made from.
The point Manufacturing removes water from natural gypsum to make the powder; mixing with water at the bench puts it back — the same reaction, run in reverse.

Clinical uses

Why dentistry needs it

  • Casts and models — a rigid, dimensionally stable replica of the arch, for study, diagnosis and treatment planning.
  • Dies — an accurate replica of a single prepared tooth (one cut down by the dentist to receive a crown), on which the crown or bridge is built.
  • Mounting — fixing casts onto an articulator (a mechanical device that reproduces how the upper and lower jaws bite together) so a restoration can be built to fit that bite outside the mouth.
  • Investing — embedding a shaped wax pattern in gypsum before casting molten metal around it, so the metal takes the wax’s shape once the wax burns out.
The point The impression itself (Lecture 08) exists only long enough to be poured; the gypsum product is what survives on the bench afterwards.
Part 2 of 6
02

Manufacture sets the type

β- vs α-hemihydrate· classification

Manufacture

Same dihydrate, two different hemihydrates

β-hemihydrate (plaster)

  • Heated in an open vessel, at atmospheric pressure, to drive off water.
  • Water leaves quickly and unevenly.
  • Particles: porous, irregular, fused together.

α-hemihydrate (stone)

  • Heated in an autoclave, under steam pressure, to drive off water.
  • Water leaves in a controlled, uniform way.
  • Particles: dense, regular, largely non-porous.
The point Same chemical formula, different particle architecture — and particle architecture is what the rest of this lecture traces forward.

FIG 09.1 Open-vessel calcination yields porous β-hemihydrate; autoclaving under steam pressure yields dense α-hemihydrate.

Structure → property

Particle shape sets how much water is needed

Mechanism Porous, irregular β particles trap water in their internal spaces and need more of it to wet and flow; dense, regular α particles pack efficiently and need less.
Practical consequence Typical water/powder ratios run roughly 0.45–0.60 for plaster and 0.28–0.32 for stone — illustrative, manufacturer-specific figures, not an ISO requirement.

Ranges reported across dental-materials textbooks (Van Noort ch. 3.1; Anusavice); exact values are manufacturer-specific and fall outside ISO 6873.

FIG 09.2 In β, water sits both inside the pores and between the particles; in α, only between them.

Part 3 of 6
03

The setting reaction

Dissolution → precipitation

Mechanism

Dissolution, then precipitation

Dissolution

Hemihydrate powder dissolves into the mixing water — it is markedly more soluble than the dihydrate it will become.

Supersaturation

As more hemihydrate dissolves, the solution becomes supersaturated with calcium sulphate relative to the much lower solubility of the dihydrate.

Precipitation

Dihydrate crystallises out of the supersaturated solution: needle-like crystals nucleate, grow, and interlock into a rigid mass.

The point CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O + heat — the reaction is measurably exothermic.

Controlling the set

Setting time· water/powder ratio

Controlling the set

What speeds up or slows down setting

  • Particle size — finer powder dissolves faster (more surface area), shortening setting time.
  • Spatulation — mixing breaks nascent crystals into more nucleation sites, which speeds the set.
  • Water/powder ratio — more water dilutes the solution and lengthens setting time (next slide).
  • Chemical additives — accelerators (e.g. potassium sulphate) and retarders (e.g. borax) exist and are used to fine-tune setting time; their individual mechanisms are outside this lecture’s scope.

Van Noort, ch. 3.1.

The master variable

Water/powder ratio: effect on setting time and strength

Mechanism A more dilute mix takes longer to become supersaturated, so setting time lengthens. It also leaves more space once the mixing water evaporates, raising porosity and lowering strength once set.
Important qualification More water does not make the mix more forgiving — it makes the finished cast weaker and slower to set, the inverse of what an easier-to-pour mix suggests.

Trend reported for Type III/IV gypsum, e.g. Alsadi et al., “Influence of Water Type and Water/Powder Ratio on the Strength and Hardness of Type III and IV Gypsum”, BJSTR.

Part 5 of 6
05

Expansion — a controlled side effect

Setting expansion· hygroscopic expansion

Setting expansion

Setting expansion: crystal growth in air

Setting expansion
A small linear expansion of the cast that occurs as the interlocking dihydrate crystals grow, measured in air.
Mechanism As dihydrate crystals nucleate throughout the mass and grow toward each other, their outward growth pushes neighbouring crystals apart before mutual contact stops it — comparable to a frost pattern forming.

ISO 6873:2013, clause 5.4; mechanism: Van Noort, ch. 3.1.

A larger effect

Hygroscopic expansion: setting against water

Hygroscopic expansion
The much larger expansion that occurs when the same setting reaction proceeds while immersed in, or in contact with, free water, rather than in air.
Mechanism In air, the surface tension of the small amount of residual mixing water holds the growing crystals back. Free water on the surface removes that restraint, so the same crystals push further before they lock together — which is why hygroscopic expansion is several times larger than setting expansion in air.

FIG 09.7 In air, residual water’s surface tension restrains crystal growth; free water on the surface removes that restraint.

Clinical significance

Clinical significance: expansion as shrinkage compensation

Too little expansion

  • The cast comes out smaller than the mould that produced it.
  • A crown or metal framework cast to that measurement will not seat correctly.

Too much expansion

  • The cast comes out oversized — the same mismatch, in the opposite direction.
  • The gypsum expands more than intended when nothing restrains it.
Practical consequence ISO 6873 sets a range, not a minimum — up to 0.20% for Type 3 (dental stone) and up to 0.15% for Type 4 (die stone) at 2 hours: the same limits already met when an overly flexible impression let a gypsum cast expand unrestrained (Lecture 08).

What controls it

Factors controlling strength

  • Water/powder ratio — the dominant factor: less water packs more dihydrate crystal per unit volume.
  • Porosity — voids left by excess water, or air trapped during mixing, weaken the set mass.
  • Spatulation time — more mixing creates more, smaller crystals, which can raise strength but also speeds setting.

Synthesis

Plaster and stone, side by side

Plaster (β, Types 1–2)

  • Porous, irregular particles.
  • Higher water/powder ratio.
  • Lower strength, lower cost.

Stone (α, Types 3–5)

  • Dense, regular particles.
  • Lower water/powder ratio.
  • Higher strength, higher cost.

FIG 09.8 Particle morphology explains the water demand and strength gap between plaster and stone.

Take-home

Gypsum products — the essentials

1
Type follows manufacture. β-hemihydrate (open-air calcination, porous particles) gives the plasters (Types 1–2); α-hemihydrate (autoclave, dense particles) gives the stones (Types 3–5).
2
Setting is dissolution then precipitation. Hemihydrate dissolves, the solution supersaturates, and interlocking dihydrate crystals precipitate and lock the mass rigid.
3
Water/powder ratio governs the trade-off. Less water gives a denser, stronger, less porous cast; more water gives the opposite. Setting and hygroscopic expansion, kept within the ISO 6873 range, compensate shrinkage elsewhere in the workflow.
