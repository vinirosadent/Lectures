The building blocks

Polymer · monomer · mer· Homo/copolymer· Molecular weight · distribution

Core vocabulary

Polymer, monomer, mer

Monomer (mer)
The smallest repeating unit. Mono = single; mer from Greek meros = part.
Polymer
A long-chain organic molecule made of many repeating units linked together.
Oligomer
A short polymer — up to ~10 mers (e.g. siloxane oligomers in impression silicones).

FIG. One monomer → many repeating units linked into a chain.

Core vocabulary

The polymerization reaction · homopolymers

Polymerization
The reaction by which monomer units become chemically linked into a high-molecular-weight molecule.
Homopolymer
Built from one type of monomer only — for example polyethylene or PMMA. It can be arranged as a single straight chain (linear) or with side branches.

FIG. A homopolymer — one monomer, arranged linear or branched.

Core vocabulary

The polymerization reaction · copolymers

Copolymer
Built from more than one type of monomer — this is how chemists "tailor-make" properties (e.g., Bis-GMA + TEGDMA in composites).
  • The two monomers can be arranged randomly, in blocks, or as a graft (side chains of one on a backbone of another).
  • Changing the ratio and arrangement lets you tune stiffness, viscosity and handling — we return to Bis-GMA + TEGDMA in Part 5.

FIG. Random, block and graft copolymers — same components, different arrangement.

Origin

Natural vs synthetic polymers

Natural polymers
The basic constituents of living matter — proteins, polysaccharides (starch), polynucleic acids (DNA).
Synthetic polymers
Made in the laboratory by chemical reactions — the ones we use clinically.
  • Dental examples: acrylic resins, elastic impression materials, and the vinyl polymers derived from ethylene (C₂H₄).
Bridge Alginate is a modified natural polymer (from seaweed); PMMA and composites are fully synthetic. Both obey the same chain rules we build today.

FIG. Natural sources (seaweed, proteins) vs synthetic (lab) — both give polymer chains.

Worked example · degree of polymerization

Degree of polymerization — a worked example

Let's calculate both quantities for a real monomer, one step at a time.

Start with the monomer

Ethylene, CH₂=CH₂, has a molecular weight of 28 g/mol.

Choose a chain length

Suppose n = 5 monomer units join together into one chain.

Multiply

MWpolymer = 28 × 5 = 140 g/mol. The degree of polymerization, DP, is simply the number of units: DP = n = 5.

The two formulas are the same idea MWpolymer = MWmer × n and DP = MWpolymer ÷ MWmer — DP just tells you how many mers are in the chain. A bigger DP means a longer chain (Part 2).

FIG. CH₂=CH₂ (28 g/mol), n = 5 → 140 g/mol.

Structure → property

Why longer chains mean stronger material

  • Polymers of large molecules are stronger and more resistant to thermal and mechanical stress than those of small ones.
  • Longer chains form more entanglements — physically harder to pull apart or distort.
  • So strength and rigidity both rise with chain length.
Mental model Short chains slide like loose spaghetti; long, entangled chains grip one another.

FIG. Short chains slide apart; long chains entangle and resist.

Structure → property

Molecular-weight distribution

  • Chain growth is random — some chains grow faster, some stop early. Not all chains are the same length.
  • Each chain has its own MW and DP, so a polymer's MW is reported as an average.
  • The distribution (fraction of low-, medium-, high-MW chains) affects properties as much as the average.
  • Two polymers with the same average MW can behave differently — one of long chains, one of many short ones.

FIG. A spread of chain lengths; same average MW can hide different distributions.

How the chains are arranged

Linear · branched · cross-linked· Thermoplastic vs thermosetting

Architecture

Linear and branched chains

Linear
Repeating units connected one after another in a single, unbranched sequence — like one long strand of spaghetti.
Branched
A main chain with one or more side branches coming off it — like a strand of spaghetti with extra pieces tied on partway along.
  • Both are still held to their neighbouring chains only by weak secondary bonds (van der Waals, hydrogen bonding) — there is no covalent bridge between separate chains yet.
  • Why the distinction matters: branching disrupts how tightly chains can pack together, which changes density and how easily neighbouring chains entangle — directly affecting the strength and stiffness we just discussed.
  • Because those inter-chain bonds are weak, linear and branched polymers can still slide past each other, soften with heat, and dissolve in solvents — this is exactly what makes a polymer thermoplastic (next slides).

FIG. Linear vs branched homopolymer — same monomer, different connectivity.

Architecture

Cross-linked networks

  • Adjacent chains joined at points by covalent (primary) bonds — the cross-links.
  • This turns separate chains into a single 3-D network.
  • Built with multi-functional monomers (dimethacrylates like Bis-GMA) carrying ≥2 reactive double bonds.
  • The network cannot flow or dissolve — the basis of composite and denture-tooth durability.

FIG. Cross-linked network: monomer units (light), chains (grey), covalent cross-links (dark).

Thermal behaviour

Thermoplastic polymers

  • Can be shaped by heat and keep shape on cooling — soften when heated, harden when cooled, reversibly.
  • Chains held only by weak secondary bonds (van der Waals, hydrogen bonding) that break with heat.
  • Relatively soft; sensitive to temperature; dissolve in organic solvents.
  • Example: non-cross-linked PMMA.
Clinical link Thermoplastic behaviour lets a clear aligner be thermoformed — and is also why it creeps and distorts in hot water.

Thermal behaviour

Thermosetting polymers

  • Take a permanent shape set by a chemical reaction — a cross-linked 3-D network of covalent bonds.
  • Cannot be re-melted or reformed; excess heat degrades/chars rather than melts them.
  • Cross-links restrict movement, so the material holds shape under load and heat.
  • Example: cured composite resin and cross-linked denture teeth.
One line Thermoplastic = secondary bonds, reversible. Thermoset = covalent network, permanent. Bond type dictates clinical behaviour.
Part 4 of 6

How polymers form

What polymerization is· Addition vs condensation· Free-radical stages · consequences

Setting the scene

What is polymerization — and which kind matters here?

  • The idea. Polymerization is simply joining many small monomers into one large chain. Everything after this is how that joining happens.
  • Two great families. Addition (chain-growth) and condensation (step-growth) — they differ in speed, in whether a by-product is released, and in dimensional stability.
  • Our focus. Most direct dental materials — composites, PMMA — cure by free-radical addition. That is what we detail next, because it explains setting, shrinkage and residual monomer.
Roadmap First we compare the two families side by side. Then we look closely at free-radical addition — the specific reaction that cures composites and PMMA — one stage at a time: initiation, propagation, termination.

Family 1 · addition (chain-growth)

Addition polymerization

  • Unsaturated monomers with C=C double bonds link together, repeating the same structure — without forming any by-product.
  • Fast reaction; monomers add only onto active chain ends, so high molecular weight forms almost immediately.
  • No small molecule is released during the reaction — unlike condensation. That does not mean every monomer molecule reacts: some are always left behind (Part 4, later).
  • Examples: poly(ethylene), poly(acrylic acid), PMMA, composite resins, addition silicone (PVS).

FIG. Monomer + monomer → chain. No by-product formed.

Family 2 · condensation (step-growth)

Condensation polymerization

  • Functional groups react and form a covalent bond while splitting off a small by-product — water or alcohol — at every linkage.
  • Step-wise and slow: any two reactive molecules can join — two monomers, a monomer and an oligomer, or two oligomers — so monomer disappears early into many small oligomers, and high molecular weight only appears near the very end.
  • High MW is reached only near the very end of the reaction.
  • Because the by-product keeps evaporating, dimensional stability over time is poor.
  • Dental examples: polysulfides, polyethers, and condensation silicones.

FIG. HO–R–OH + HOOC–R′–COOH → polymer + water (the by-product).

Family 1 vs family 2

Addition vs condensation, side by side

One question separates them clinically: is a small molecule released at every link?

Addition (chain-growth)

  • By-product: none — no small molecule is released.
  • Speed: rapid; long chains form early.
  • Monomer left over: falls gradually as curing proceeds.
  • Stays accurate over time: yes — only shrinks during curing.

Condensation (step-growth)

  • By-product: a small molecule (water, ethanol) at every link.
  • Speed: slow, stepwise; long chains only at the end.
  • Monomer left over: used up early into short oligomers.
  • Stays accurate over time: no — keeps shrinking as by-product evaporates.
Clinical anchor A condensation silicone impression must be poured immediately — its ethanol by-product keeps evaporating, and the impression keeps shrinking. PVS (addition) releases nothing, so it stays accurate and can be poured days later.

Free-radical addition

The reaction in three stages

Composites and PMMA cure by free-radical addition of methacrylate monomers — a chain reaction in three stages. A free radical is a molecule with an unpaired electron, so it reacts eagerly with a nearby double bond. We now take each stage on its own.

FIG. Overview — initiation → chain start → propagation → termination. The next three slides expand each stage.

Stage 1 · initiation

Making the first radical

  • An initiator must be activated — by heat, light, or a chemical redox reaction — before it can release free radicals.
  • Classic chemical initiator: benzoyl peroxide, which splits (homolytic cleavage) into two radicals.
  • In light-cured resins: camphorquinone absorbs blue light (~468 nm) and, with an amine co-initiator, generates radicals.
  • A radical (R•) meets a C=C double bond, pairs with one electron and leaves the other unpaired — so the monomer itself becomes the new radical, ready to grow.

FIG. Initiator activated → radicals → attack a monomer double bond.

Stage 2 · propagation

The chain grows — fast

  • The active monomer radical attacks the double bond of the next monomer, opening it and adding it to the chain.
  • The radical site shifts to the new chain end, and the process repeats — again and again, within milliseconds.
  • As chains lengthen, the mix thickens (viscosity rises) and begins to stiffen.
  • The reaction is strongly exothermic and continues until nearly all monomer is consumed.
Sequence R–M• + M → R–MM• + M → R–MMM• … the radical always sits at the growing tip.

FIG. The radical hops to each new chain end as monomers add — fast and exothermic.

Stage 3 · termination

How growth stops

Combination (direct coupling)

Two radical chain ends collide and join into one longer chain.

Disproportionation

A radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from another growing chain; both become inactive.

Trapping

Remaining double bonds get locked in the vitrifying network and can no longer meet — the origin of residual monomer.

FIG. Detailed chemistry — benzoyl peroxide initiation, propagation, termination by combination.

Measuring the reaction · degree of conversion

Degree of conversion: how much really reacted?

Degree of conversion0%
Degree of conversion (DC)
The percentage of C=C double bonds that have actually reacted (become C–C single bonds) by the end of cure. Measured by FTIR, comparing the double-bond signal before and after curing.

No light yet — every double bond (red) is still unreacted.

Notice it climbs fast, then flattens — it never reaches 100%. Some double bonds get trapped in the rigid network before they can find a partner (Termination, previous slide).

Consequence 1 · heat

Polymerization gives off heat

  • Forming covalent bonds releases energy, so the reaction is exothermic.
  • In bulk (e.g. curing a large PMMA denture), this exotherm can be significant and must be managed.
  • If the temperature runs too high, monomer can boil and leave porosity in the set material.
  • It is an inherent consequence of chain growth — not a defect to be "fixed", but a process to be controlled.
Clinical note Heat-cured denture processing uses a slow, staged temperature cycle precisely to control this exotherm and avoid porosity.

Consequence 2 · shrinkage

The material contracts as it sets

  • As monomers convert to a polymer, they pack closer and volume is lost — polymerization shrinkage.
  • It is inherent to turning loosely spaced monomers into a tightly bonded network.
  • The contraction itself is tolerable; the problem is the stress it generates once the material can no longer flow.
  • This drives stress at the tooth–restoration interface — developed fully in Part 6, and the key to our failing composite.

FIG. Monomers pack closer on curing — the volume lost is the shrinkage.

Consequence 3 · residual monomer

Not every monomer reacts

As the resin vitrifies, the last double bonds can no longer meet — this is exactly why degree of conversion never reaches 100%, leaving unreacted residual monomer trapped in the matrix. Three consequences:

Mechanical degradation

Disrupts the network, lowering hardness and flexural strength.

Plasticization

Free monomer increases free volume, softening the matrix and making it more prone to deform.

Biocompatibility

Mobile monomer leaches into saliva — cytotoxic to pulp, a cause of allergy.

FIG. Some double bonds stay trapped and unreacted, then leach into saliva.

Surface effect · oxygen

The oxygen-inhibited layer

  • Atmospheric oxygen diffuses into the surface of setting resin and reacts with propagating radicals faster than monomer does.
  • This forms stable, unreactive peroxyl radicals that cannot continue the chain.
  • Result: a thin, tacky, poorly polymerized, mechanically weak surface layer.
  • Useful between increments (it helps them bond); on the final surface it must be polished away to prevent wear and marginal breakdown.

FIG. O₂ diffuses into the surface and forms chain-stopping peroxyl radicals — a thin tacky layer.

What controls the properties

Cross-linking · copolymers· Plasticizers · sorption· Strengthening

Modifier · cross-linking

What cross-linking does to properties

  • Even a small degree of cross-linking limits how far chains move under stress.
  • It increases strength, hardness, rigidity and solvent resistance.
  • It decreases water sorption — a tighter mesh blocks fluid diffusion, reducing swelling and hydrolytic degradation.
  • But excessive cross-linking makes the material brittle — there is an optimum, not "more is always better".
Dental example Bis-GMA is a cross-linking dimethacrylate — it gives composites stiffness and solvent resistance.

FIG. A dense cross-linked mesh blocks water; a loose network swells.

Modifier · copolymers

Copolymers: tailor-making properties

  • Copolymers contain two or more different monomer units, letting chemists "tailor-make" predicted properties.
  • Three arrangements: random, block, and graft — see the figure on the previous slides.
  • Bis-GMA. A large, rigid dimethacrylate built on a bisphenol-A core (two bulky ring systems). That bulk makes it stiff and viscous, and gives cured composite low shrinkage and high strength — but it is too thick to use alone.
  • TEGDMA. A small dimethacrylate with a short, flexible chain between its two reactive ends. Blending it in as a copolymer dilutes the viscosity and improves handling and filler loading.
  • The trade-off. TEGDMA is smaller, so it packs more reactive double bonds per unit volume — the copolymer handles better but shrinks more (we return to this in Part 6).

Simplified schematic — not to exact chemical scale, drawn to show why one is rigid and the other is flexible.

Modifier · plasticizers

Plasticizers: making a polymer flexible

  • Small molecules that insert between the chains, pushing them apart and weakening the secondary forces that hold them.
  • This raises free volume and chain mobility, so chains slide past each other more easily.
  • Main effect: greater flexibility, as chains can now slide more easily past one another.
  • Side effects: lower hardness and strength, more creep, higher solvent uptake, and leaching over time.

FIG. Before → mechanism → effects of plasticization.

Modifier · plasticizers

External vs internal — and rigid vs flexible

  • External plasticizer. A separate small molecule that penetrates between the chains; not bonded, so it can leach out over time.
  • Internal plasticizer. Introduced by copolymerization with a suitable co-monomer — part of the main chain, so it cannot leach.
  • Both reduce strength and hardness, and make the material easier to bend.
  • Rigid vs flexible is simply how freely the chains can move at mouth temperature: a rigid polymer keeps its shape stiffly; a flexible one bends and stretches readily.
Key distinction Rigid vs flexible depends on how easily the chains slide past each other at mouth temperature — exactly what a plasticizer controls.

Plasticizers in the clinic

Where plasticizers are used — and why they fail

  • Resilient denture liners. Plasticizers (e.g. dibutyl phthalate) turn rigid PMMA into a soft cushion that spreads occlusal load over compromised mucosa.
  • Tissue conditioners. Highly plasticized systems flow under pressure to record inflamed tissue while cushioning it.
  • Clinical degradation. External plasticizers are not bonded, so they leach into saliva — the liner turns hard, brittle and rough and needs replacement.

FIG. Plasticizer cushions the liner; as it leaches out, the liner hardens.

Durability · sorption & solubility

How oral fluids get into a polymer

  • Free volume. Loosely packed, poorly cross-linked chains leave gaps that water diffuses into.
  • Chemical polarity. Hydrophilic groups (–OH in Bis-GMA, ether links in TEGDMA) hydrogen-bond with water, pulling fluid in.
  • Sorption. Absorbed water forces chains apart, swelling the material and disrupting secondary bonds — hydrolytic degradation.
  • Solubility & leaching. Unbound monomer, initiator and additives dissolve out into the mouth, weakening the material.

FIG. Water diffuses IN (sorption); soluble components leach OUT (solubility).

Design lever

How to make a polymer stronger

Four levers, all met today — and the last one bridges to the composites lecture.

  • Increase MW / DP. Longer chains, more entanglement.
  • Cross-link. A covalent network resists deformation and solvents.
  • Copolymerize. Blend monomers to tune the balance of properties.
  • Add inorganic fillers. Reinforce the matrix → a composite.

FIG. Four levers: raise MW, cross-link, copolymerize, add filler.

Shrinkage, stress & clinical failure

Molecular driver· Gel point · C-Factor· Clinical consequences

Shrinkage · 1 · molecular mechanism

Where the volume goes

This is the first time you meet shrinkage at the molecular level — take it slowly.

  • Before curing, monomers sit apart, held by loose van der Waals forces at ~3.40 Å.
  • During addition, those are replaced by short, tight covalent C–C bonds at ~1.54 Å — a −1.86 Å pull at every reaction site.
  • Monomers also lose translational freedom, letting the network pack tighter.
  • Net result: volumetric contraction, typically 1.5–6% for restorative composites.

FIG. van der Waals spacing (3.40 Å) → covalent bond (1.54 Å): −1.86 Å per site.

Shrinkage · 2 · why dental resins

Why do dental polymers shrink?

  • Direct composites and acrylics polymerize by addition, forming permanent cross-linked networks — so they always shrink as they set.
  • Bis-GMA is viscous and rigid; TEGDMA is a small, low-viscosity diluent added to improve handling and filler loading.
  • But TEGDMA is small, so it has a high concentration of double bonds per unit volume — more bonds converted means more shrinkage.
  • So more TEGDMA improves handling but increases total polymerization shrinkage — a genuine trade-off.

FIG. Small, double-bond-rich monomers pack closer after curing → greater contraction.

Shrinkage · 3 · shrinkage → stress

The gel point changes everything

Pre-gel

The resin is still fluid; contraction is relieved by plastic flow from free surfaces — no stress builds up.

Gel point

A continuous 3-D network suddenly spans the material; it turns from viscous liquid to viscoelastic solid.

Post-gel

The rigid, bonded material can no longer flow, so further contraction is converted directly into residual stress.

FIG. Stress stays near zero before the gel point, then climbs steeply after it.

Interactive · the gel point

Drag the reaction forward — watch stress appear

Interfacial stress—

The composite is bonded to two rigid cavity walls, just like inside a real tooth. Drag the slider to run the reaction forward and watch what happens once the material can no longer flow.

Still a viscous liquid — the composite flows to compensate for shrinkage, so no stress reaches the bonded walls.

Around 45% conversion, a continuous network suddenly spans the cavity — the gel point. Past it, the same shrinkage has nowhere to go: it converts directly into stress pulling on the bonded walls. This is exactly why timing (soft-start curing) and technique (incremental layering, next slides) matter.

Configuration factor

C-Factor: how cavity shape sets the stress

Definition C-Factor = bonded surfaces ÷ unbonded (free) surfaces. The more bonded walls relative to free surfaces, the less the composite can flow to relieve stress.
  • High C-Factor — Class I. 5 bonded walls : 1 free surface → C = 5. Almost no free surface to flow to, so intense stress concentrates at the interface.
  • Low C-Factor — Class V. 1 bonded wall : 5 free surfaces → C = 0.2. The composite flows freely, minimizing interface stress.
  • Your lever. You cannot change the cavity, but incremental layering lowers the effective C-Factor of each increment.

FIG. Class I (5 bonded : 1 free, C = 5) vs Class V (1 bonded : 5 free, C = 0.2).
