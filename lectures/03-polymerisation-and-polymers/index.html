Polymers & Polymerization

A long chain, built from repeating units — how it forms decides how it fails.

Full explanation — kinetics, and the PVS caveat

Addition (chain-growth): fast; monomers add only to an active chain end; high MW forms early; no by-product — 100% of mass ends up in the polymer.

Condensation (step-growth): slow, stepwise; any two functional groups can react; high MW only near completion; a small molecule is ejected at every linkage.

Addition silicones are near-perfect, but some release a little hydrogen gas from residual Si–H groups just after setting — hence the "wait before pouring" instruction on some products.

Core science · 3

Composites cure by free-radical addition — three stages.

Interactive · step through the cure

Energy splits an initiator into radicals (camphorquinone + amine, light-cured). A radical attacks a C=C bond.

The active end grabs the next monomer; the radical hops to the new end. Repeats in milliseconds.

Two radicals combine, or one steals a hydrogen — growth stops. Or the network traps the radical as it stiffens.

Full explanation — initiators, inhibitors, oxygen layer

Initiators generate the first radicals (benzoyl peroxide, heat-cure; camphorquinone + amine, light-cure). Inhibitors (hydroquinone, BHT) scavenge early radicals so the resin doesn't set on the shelf.

Atmospheric oxygen is itself an inhibitor — it reacts with surface radicals faster than monomer can, leaving a sticky, under-cured oxygen-inhibited layer. Useful between increments; must be polished off the final surface.

Full explanation — Tg, and why the trade-offs happen

The glass transition temperature (Tg) is where chains gain enough mobility to shift from glassy to rubbery. Cross-linking pushes Tg up; plasticizers pull it down. A denture base needs Tg safely above mouth and hot-drink temperatures.

Plasticizer side effects: lower strength, creep, more solvent uptake, and leaching over time. Over-cross-linking side effect: brittleness, even as solvent resistance and rigidity improve.

The payoff

Shrinkage isn't the problem. Post-gel stress is.

Interactive · the gel point decides everything

Before the gel point, the composite still flows and adapts.

◆ Back to the hero

Stress beats the bond → marginal gap → microleakage → staining, secondary caries, sensitivity. Managed with increments, C-factor, and cure rate — clinicians control when the stress develops, not the chemistry.

Full explanation — the Bis-GMA / TEGDMA trade-off

Bis-GMA is big, viscous, shrinks little — but too stiff to handle alone. TEGDMA dilutes it for handling and filler loading, but small monomers pack more double bonds per unit volume: more TEGDMA, more shrinkage. Handling and shrinkage pull in opposite directions in every composite formulation.

Check your model

Three things students usually get backwards.

Myth
"Shrinkage is the problem."
Fact
Post-gel stress is. Pre-gel shrinkage is relieved by flow.
Myth
"Residual monomer = didn't cure properly."
Fact
Degree of conversion ≠ degree of polymerization.
Myth
"More cross-linking is always better."
Fact
Excess cross-linking → brittle.

Take-home

  1. MW = MW of one mer × n. Longer chains, higher MW.
  2. Addition: no by-product. Condensation: ejects one — the root of poor stability.
  3. Free-radical cure: initiation → propagation → termination.
  4. Thermoset = covalent network, not just "harder".
  5. Cross-linking ↑ strength & Tg, risks brittleness. Plasticizer does the opposite.
  6. The clinical killer is post-gel shrinkage stress — manage it, don't fight the chemistry.
