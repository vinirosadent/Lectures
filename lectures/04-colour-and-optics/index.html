The core idea

Colour is not "in" the object

Colour is a psychophysical sensation: a physical stimulus (light) is modified by an object, captured by the eye, and interpreted by the brain. It needs three things at once.

1 · Illuminant (light)
Electromagnetic radiant energy the eye can detect, ≈ 380–780 nm. No light → no colour.
2 · Object
Its composition and microstructure selectively absorb some wavelengths and reflect/transmit others. This is the part we engineer.
3 · Observer
Retinal cones turn the returning light into nerve signals; the brain interprets them as a colour.
FigureLight · object · observerassets/light-object-observer.pngTriangle: lamp emits light → tooth modifies it → eye/brain interpret. Change any one and colour changes.

The stimulus

The visible spectrum — white light is a mixture

Visible spectrum
The band of wavelengths the eye detects (~380–780 nm). Short = violet/blue; long = orange/red.
  • White light is all visible wavelengths together (e.g. midday daylight).
  • What an object reflects/transmits back to the eye is its perceived colour: reflect all → white, absorb all → black, reflect only ~700 nm → red.
  • A prism (or the hydroxyapatite crystals of enamel) can bend each wavelength differently and split white light into its components — the basis of opalescence in Part 4.
Aditive primaries — RGB The eye has three cone types, so any colour of light can be made from Red + Green + Blue. R+G+B at full = white; this is why screens are RGB. (Mixing pigments is subtractive — a different rule.)
FigureVisible spectrum 380–780 nmassets/visible-spectrum.pngHorizontal spectrum bar with wavelength scale; call-outs for violet/blue/green/yellow/orange/red.

Light intro · the observer

How the eye turns wavelengths into colour

We keep this brief — for a materials course the observer is fixed context. But knowing why value matters most starts here.

Cones (colour) & rods (grey)
~6–7 million cones (bright light, colour, packed in the fovea) and ~120 million rods (dim light, no colour).
  • Trichromatic theory: three cone types — S (~430–446 nm, blue), M (~531–555 nm, green), L (~558–599 nm, red). Any colour is the balance of their signals.
  • Opponent-process theory: those signals are re-coded into pairs — red–green, blue–yellow, and a separate black–white (lightness) channel. That is why we never see "reddish-green".
  • The dedicated lightness channel is why the eye resolves value (lightness) far more finely than hue or chroma — remember this in Part 2.
FigureS/M/L cone responseassets/cone-response-curves.pngThree overlapping sensitivity curves (S~440, M~540, L~570 nm) across the visible spectrum.

The three attributes of colour

Hue· Value· Chroma

Describing any colour

Three numbers pin down a colour

The Munsell system (1905) showed that any colour is fixed by three independent attributes. Learn these words precisely — the rest of the lecture uses them.

Hue
The colour family (red, yellow, blue) — set by the dominant wavelength.
Value
The lightness (black→white), independent of hue.
Chroma
The saturation — how strong or pure the hue is.
FigureHue · value · chroma at a glanceassets/hvc-overview.pngOne diagram: hue wheel, a vertical value scale, and a chroma (saturation) gradient — labelled.

Attribute 1 · hue

Hue — the colour family

Hue
What everyday language calls "the colour": red, orange, yellow, green, blue. Physically it is the dominant wavelength reflected by the object.
  • What it means for teeth: natural teeth sit in a narrow band — the yellow to yellow-red hues. You will never match a tooth with a "blue" or "green" hue.
  • Example — Vita Classical guide: the letter is the hue → A = reddish-brown, B = reddish-yellow, C = greyish, D = reddish-grey. The number after it is chroma.
  • Get hue wrong and the tooth reads the wrong "family" — but hue is usually the easiest of the three to match, because teeth vary little in hue.
FigureTooth hues (A/B/C/D)assets/hue-shade-families.pngThe Vita A–D hue families as swatches, with the natural-tooth yellow/yellow-red zone highlighted on a hue wheel.

Attribute 2 · value

Value — lightness, and the one that matters most

Value
The relative lightness or darkness of a colour on a grey scale from 0 (black) to 10 (white), independent of hue.
  • Why it dominates: the eye's dedicated black–white channel resolves lightness far more finely than hue or chroma. A value mismatch is the first thing that looks wrong.
  • What low value looks like: a tooth with low value absorbs more light and appears dark, grey, "non-vital".
  • Clinical technique: squint when judging value — it suppresses colour vision and isolates lightness.
  • Rule of thumb: when unsure, pick the higher value (lighter) — a technician can lower value with stain, but cannot easily raise it. Vita 3D-Master even asks you to match value first.
FigureValue scale 0–10assets/value-scale.pngVertical grey scale 0(black)→10(white); a tooth placed on it; a low-value "greyish non-vital" tooth beside a high-value one.

Attribute 3 · chroma

Chroma — saturation, and its link to value

Chroma
The intensity / purity of a hue — how far the colour departs from a neutral grey of the same value. Think of adding dye to water: the hue stays, the strength climbs.
  • What drives it in teeth: chroma is set mainly by the dentine (the coloured core), while enamel is nearly colourless.
  • Cervical vs incisal: chroma is highest at the neck (thin enamel, dentine shows) and lowest at the translucent incisal edge.
  • The key coupling — value and chroma move inversely: raising chroma packs in more colourant, which absorbs more light, so the tooth also reads darker (lower value).
FigureChroma steps + inverse valueassets/chroma-scale.pngA row of the same hue at rising chroma (A1→A4), with an arrow showing value dropping as chroma rises.

How light interacts with matter

Reflect · absorb · scatter · transmit· Refractive index· Translucency

Structure → property (the core)

Light meeting a material can only do four things

Every optical property in this lecture is just a mix of these four fates. The proportion of each is set by the material's microstructure — this is the hinge from structure to property.

FigureThe four fates of lightassets/four-fates-of-light.pngOne incident white ray hitting a material block, splitting into labelled reflection, absorption, scattering and transmission — wide landscape layout.
R
Reflection — bounces off the surface (gloss).
A
Absorption — taken up inside → creates hue.
S
Scattering — redirected inside → cloudy/opaque.
T
Transmission — passes through → translucency.

Fate 1 & 2 · the surface story

Reflection & absorption — gloss and hue

What happens at the surface and to specific wavelengths — these two fates give a material its shine and its colour family.

Reflection — specular vs diffuse
Specular = a sharp highlight off a smooth, polished surface → high gloss (think glazed ceramic). Diffuse = a soft glow off a rough surface, which also raises apparent value by bouncing light back.
Absorption = the origin of hue
Pigments/chromophores swallow specific wavelengths; the leftover reflected wavelengths are the hue you see. Absorb all → black; absorb none → white; absorb blue → the object looks yellow.
Example · why polish matters A well-polished composite reflects specularly and looks vital and glossy; leave it rough and it scatters at the surface, dulls the gloss and shifts apparent value — a handling issue, not just aesthetics.
FigureSpecular vs diffuse + selective absorptionassets/reflection-specular-diffuse.pngTop: smooth surface → one sharp reflected ray (gloss) vs rough → many scattered rays (matte). Bottom: white light hitting a yellow object, blue wavelengths absorbed, yellow reflected.

Fate 3 & 4 · the body story

Scattering & transmission — inside the material

What happens to light after it enters — these two fates decide whether a material is opaque or translucent.

Scattering
Light is redirected in many directions at internal interfaces (particles, crystals, pores). Lots of scattering → light bounces back before it can pass through → the material looks cloudy / opaque and lighter.
Transmission
Light travels through to the other side with little scattering → the material is translucent and shows depth (and lets the background influence its colour).
Example · enamel vs dentine Enamel scatters little → translucent; dentine scatters strongly (tubules, protein) → more opaque. A ceramist stacks a translucent "enamel" layer over an opaque "dentine" layer to copy this.
FigureScattering vs transmissionassets/scattering-transmission.pngTwo blocks: one with many particles scattering light back (opaque, cloudy); one clear block letting light pass straight through (translucent), with a background shape visible through it.

The master variable · what it is

What is refractive index?

Before we use it, let's be clear what "refractive index" actually means — it is the single most useful number for predicting how a material handles light.

Refractive index (RI, n)
How much a material slows and bends light: n = c / v (speed of light in vacuum ÷ speed in the material). Vacuum = 1.00; water = 1.33; glass ≈ 1.5. Higher n → light travels slower and bends more.
  • Everyday analogy: a straw looks "broken" in a glass of water because light bends as it crosses from water (1.33) to air (1.00) — different n, different path.
  • The key rule: light is bent and partly scattered at every interface where n changes. No change in n → light sails straight through, unseen.
Why this matters A material is made of many tiny internal interfaces (particles, crystals, pores). Whether light passes or scatters at each one depends entirely on how well the RIs match.
FigureLight bending & the straw illusionassets/refractive-index-bending.pngLeft: a ray bending as it crosses air→water→glass with n values labelled. Right: the classic "bent straw" in a glass of water.

The master variable · in dentistry

RI match → translucent · RI mismatch → opaque

Now apply it. The same rule explains enamel, dentine, composites, ceramics and even caries — all through one number.

  • Matched RIs across internal interfaces → light passes straight → translucent.
  • Mismatched RIs (or pores) → strong scattering → opaque / white.
  • Composite, done on purpose: Bis-GMA matrix ≈ 1.54 is matched to silica/glass filler ≈ 1.53 → light passes → the composite looks translucent like enamel. Drift them apart and it turns chalky.
  • Zirconia: tiny crystals with mismatched optics scatter strongly → naturally opaque (great strength, but needs engineering to make it translucent).
  • Enamel also scatters short (blue) wavelengths more strongly (~1/λ³) — the seed of opalescence in Part 4.
The numbers to remember Enamel n ≈ 1.63 · dentine ≈ 1.54 · water 1.33 · air 1.00. A caries lesion swaps mineral for water/air → huge RI mismatch → the white-spot lesion (next slide).
FigureRI match vs mismatchassets/refractive-index-match.pngTwo panels: matched n (ray passes → translucent) vs mismatched n / porosity (ray scatters in all directions → opaque/white).

Structure → property · worked case

Case: the white-spot lesion

Sound enamel

Densely packed hydroxyapatite; internal RIs are uniform → little scattering → enamel looks translucent.

Early caries

Acid dissolves mineral, opening microscopic pores filled with water or air (n ≈ 1.33 or ≈ 1.0).

RI discontinuity

Now light hits many n-mismatched interfaces → scattering jumps → the area turns chalky white and opaque: a white-spot lesion.

Same principle, reversed Rehydrate or remineralise and the pores fill/close, the RI mismatch drops, and the lesion becomes less visible — the exact same optics that make an air-dried lesion stand out.

Optical properties of teeth & materials

Translucency · opacity· Fluorescence· Opalescence

From the four fates

Four appearance properties, one origin

Each of the four properties we meet next is just the four fates made visible — and each is something a material must reproduce to look like a tooth.

T
Translucency — light passes/diffuses through the body (transmission ≫ scatter). Enamel, incisal edge, glass ceramics.
O
Opacity — light is scattered/absorbed and the background is hidden (scatter + absorption). Dentine, opaquers, zirconia.
F
Fluorescence — absorbs UV and re-emits visible blue ~440–450 nm (absorb → re-emit). Dentine ≈ 3× enamel.
◐
Opalescence — bluish in reflected light, orange in transmitted light (wavelength-selective scatter). Enamel incisal halo.

Property · translucency & opacity

Translucency vs opacity — a gradient, not a switch

Translucency
The fraction of light that passes/diffuses through — between fully transparent and fully opaque. Measured as a Translucency Parameter (TP) or contrast ratio.
Opacity
The opposite end — light is scattered/absorbed so the background cannot show through.
  • Materials ladder: feldspathic/glass ceramics & enamel shades are translucent; dentine shades, opaquers and zirconia are more opaque.
  • Trade-off: more translucency gives lifelike depth but lowers value (less light reflected back) and lets the background through; more opacity masks the background but can look flat and "dead".
FigureTranslucency ↔ opacity gradientassets/translucency-opacity.pngA strip from transparent → translucent → opaque over a checker background, showing how much background shows through.

Property · fluorescence

Fluorescence — teeth glow blue under UV

Fluorescence
Absorbing invisible UV and re-emitting it as visible light — in teeth, a soft blue at ≈ 440–450 nm.
  • Where: both tissues fluoresce, but dentine ≈ 3× the enamel, because it is tied to dentine's organic content.
  • What it does optically: adds returned light → raises value, masks discolouration, keeps the tooth looking "alive" under daylight/UV-rich light.
  • Materials consequence: a non-fluorescent restoration stays dark while the tooth glows — a metameric failure that shows up in nightclub UV or photographs. So ceramics/composites are dosed with fluorescent agents on purpose.
FigureFluorescence under UVassets/fluorescence-uv.pngTooth under UV emitting blue (dentine brighter than enamel); a non-fluorescent restoration staying dark.

Property · opalescence

Opalescence — two colours from one enamel

Opalescence
A material that looks one colour in reflected light and another in transmitted light, like an opal. Enamel's hydroxyapatite crystals act as tiny prisms.
  • Reflected light → bluish: short (blue) wavelengths scatter most (~1/λ³) and bounce back — the enamel takes a cool blue-white cast, seen as the incisal halo.
  • Transmitted light → orange: long (orange/red) wavelengths pass through to the dentine and reflect back warm — counter-opalescence near the mamelons.
  • Materials consequence: ceramists add opalescent glasses so restorations gain the same optical depth and vitality; without it, incisal edges look flat and grey.
FigureOptical anatomy + incisal haloassets/tooth-optical-anatomy.pngIncisor cross-section: translucent opalescent enamel over opaque fluorescent dentine; blue incisal halo, orange counter-opalescence.

Measuring & communicating colour

Munsell· CIELAB & ΔE· Instruments

Why bother

Perception is subjective — so we measure

The same tooth can be judged differently by two clinicians — or by the same clinician twice. To specify, compare and reproduce colour reliably, we need numbers.

  • Visual matching is swayed by lighting, background, eye fatigue, age and experience.
  • A colour order system assigns coordinates so colour can be recorded, sent to a lab, and checked objectively.
  • Two systems dominate dentistry: Munsell (visual, chip-based) and CIELAB (instrumental, numeric).
Materials angle Every manufacturer claim of "excellent colour stability" or "perfect match" is ultimately a ΔE number under stated conditions. Knowing the scale lets you read the claim critically.
FigureSources of visual errorassets/shade-matching-conditions.pngIcons: lighting/CRI, background colour, eye fatigue, surrounding colours — the variables that bias a visual match.

System 1 · Munsell

The Munsell system — colour by chips

Munsell (1905)
Orders colour in three visually-equal steps: Hue · Value · Chroma, written as H V/C (e.g. 5YR 7/4). Built from physical colour chips.
  • Pros: intuitive, worldwide, matches how we perceive colour; the basis of visual guides like the Vita Classical.
  • Cons: relies on the eye → observer bias, lighting and fatigue; limited by real pigment chips.
FigureMunsell colour solidassets/munsell-solid.pngValue on the vertical axis, hue around the circumference, chroma along the radius.

System 2 · CIELAB

CIELAB — colour as three coordinates

CIE L*a*b* (1976)
A uniform instrumental space where distance ≈ perceived difference. L* = lightness (0–100), a* = green(−)/red(+), b* = blue(−)/yellow(+).
  • Why it replaced CIE 1931 (XYZ): the older space was not perceptually uniform — equal numeric steps did not look equally different.
  • A tooth typically sits around L*≈76–80, a*≈1–3, b*≈16–20 (light, slightly red, quite yellow).
  • Its superpower: any two colours differ by a single number — ΔE.
FigureCIELAB axesassets/cielab-axes.pngL* vertical (0→100), a* green–red, b* blue–yellow, with a sample tooth point plotted.

The number that matters

ΔE — how different is "different"?

ΔE (colour difference)
The distance between two colours in CIELAB: ΔEab = √(ΔL*² + Δa*² + Δb*²). Newer ΔE00 (CIEDE2000) corrects it to match vision even better.
  • Perceptibility threshold ≈ ΔEab 1.2 (ΔE00 0.8): below this, 50% of observers see no difference.
  • Acceptability threshold ≈ ΔEab 2.7 (ΔE00 1.8): below this, most people accept the match even if they can see it.
Reading a claim "Colour change after ageing ΔE = 1.9" means: visible, but still within the acceptable band (Paravina 2015 thresholds). Numbers turn marketing into evidence.
FigureΔE threshold rulerassets/delta-e-thresholds.pngTwo swatches diverging left→right with markers at ΔE 1.2 (perceptibility) and 2.7 (acceptability).

Light intro · instruments

How the numbers are captured

Colorimeter

  • 3–4 filtered sensors read light in R/G/B bands — mimics the eye (tristimulus).
  • Fast, cheap, portable; great for quick checks.
  • Sees no full spectrum → can be fooled by metamerism.

Spectrophotometer

  • Measures reflected energy across the whole spectrum in 1–25 nm steps.
  • Gives the full reflectance curve → most accurate, metamerism-aware.
  • Slower and costlier — the research/QC reference.
Metamerism Two colours that match under one light and differ under another. A colorimeter cannot warn you; a spectrophotometer can, because it sees the whole curve. This is why shade-matching light quality (≈5500 K, CRI > 90) matters.

What sets the final colour

Thickness · background· Surface· Ageing & stability

Property → performance

The shade tab is a label — the final colour is contextual

1
Composition — filler type/size and RI set the baseline translucency and opacity.
2
Thickness — a thin translucent layer lets the background through; a thick one masks it.
3
Background / substrate — a dark stump, metal post or discoloured dentine drags the final colour down.
4
Surface & gloss — polish raises specular reflection and apparent value; roughness dulls both.
5
Ageing — sorption, staining and matrix breakdown shift colour over time.
→
Same tab, different result — colour is a performance outcome.

Performance · stability

Colour stability — matching is not forever

Colour stability
How well a material keeps its colour over time, reported as a rising ΔE against baseline after ageing.
  • Extrinsic staining: coffee, tea, red wine, chlorhexidine adsorb onto/into the surface — worse on rough or matte surfaces.
  • Intrinsic change: water sorption and degradation of the resin matrix (and amine photo-initiators yellowing) raise chroma and drop value.
  • Design lever: higher filler load, better polish and hydrolytically stable matrices → lower ΔE over years.
FigureColour change over timeassets/colour-ageing.pngTimeline of a swatch yellowing with coffee/tea/wear icons and a rising ΔE curve vs baseline.

Common misconception

Myth vs fact

Myth

  • "If the shade tab matches, the restoration will match."
  • "Colour is a fixed property of the material."
  • "More translucent always looks better."

Fact

  • The tab fixes H/V/C of a standard chip; the real colour depends on thickness, substrate, surface, illuminant and ageing.
  • Colour is an emergent property of microstructure interacting with light and context.
  • Translucency lowers value and reveals the background — a trade-off, not a virtue.

Take-home & recap

Six ideas to keep· Where you learned each outcome

Take-home messages

The six things to remember

  • Colour is a three-body event — light × object × observer; we engineer the object, and metamerism is what happens when the light changes.
  • Three numbers describe any colour — hue, value, chroma — and the eye judges value most sharply (value ↔ chroma inverse).
  • Four fates of light — reflect, absorb, scatter, transmit — set by refractive-index mismatch, generate every appearance property.
  • Translucency, opacity, fluorescence, opalescence are those fates made visible; materials add fluorescence/opalescence on purpose.
  • We measure colour with Munsell (visual) and CIELAB (ΔE); perceptibility ≈ 1.2, acceptability ≈ 2.7 (ΔEab).
  • Final colour is contextual — thickness, background, surface and ageing move it, so "shade match" is a claim to interrogate.
